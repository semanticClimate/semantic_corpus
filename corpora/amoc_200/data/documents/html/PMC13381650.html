Centennial-scale intensifications of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation during Heinrich Stadial 1

Received: 21 February 2025

Accepted: 7 May 2026

Check for updates

Partha Sarathi Jena 1 , CristianoMazur Chiessi 1 , Ines Beese 2 , Martin Butzin 2,4 , Bruna Borba Dias 1 , Marília de Carvalho Campos 3 , Gerrit Lohmann 2 & StefanMulitza 2

Centennial-scale changes in Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) strength might disturb global climate by altering interhemispheric heat transport and CO2 partitioning between the ocean and the atmosphere. Due to the short instrumental record and lack of high-resolution paleo records that so far only resolve millennial-scale changes, centennial-scale changes remain elusive. Here we use radiocarbon ventilation ages from a western equatorial Atlantic sediment core with a remarkably high sedimentation rate to reconstruct AMOC variability during the last deglaciation, with a focus on Heinrich Stadial 1 (HS1; 17.8-14.8 ka). Results frommodel simulations indicate that ventilation ages serve as a sensitive proxy for AMOC variability under weak overturning conditions (i.e., during HS1) at our study location. Notably, within an overall weakened AMOC condition during HS1, our record shows two centennial-scale AMOC intensifications: one from 16.5 to 15.8 ka and another at ~15.4 ka. These centennial-scale episodes of intensified AMOC briefly revitalized Atlantic interhemispheric heat transport during HS1, resulting in decreased precipitation over northeastern Brazil and short-lived but intense changes in climate elsewhere. These episodes of AMOC intensification likely transported substantial volumes of CO2-rich water from the mid-depth Atlantic to the Southern Ocean, where the CO2 was rapidly outgassed to the atmosphere.

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), characterized by deepwater formation in the North Atlantic and its subsequent southward flow, is crucial for regulating global climate by transporting heat and nutrients across the Atlantic Ocean 1 . It plays a key role in the climate system by sequestering carbon and modulating climate variability 2 . Heinrich Stadials (HS), characterized by marked slowdowns of the AMOC 3 , can provide valuable insights into the possible impacts of a future collapse of the AMOC 4 . Several studies suggest that HS1 (17.8-14.8 ka) was not an episode of stable environmental conditions, but rather a period characterized by complex climate changes 5,6 . For example, a record of ice-rafted debris (IRD; evidence of melted icebergs and freshwater discharge) from the Iberian Margin shows two distinct peaks, and both peaks vary from each other in terms of the composition of the IRD deposits 5 . On the Laurentian Fan, two distinct ice rafting events have also been identified within HS1 6 . This complex climatic evolution during HS1 has been recorded in a few proxy reconstructions worldwide (e.g., Refs. 7-11), as for example, high-

Variations in heat transport linked to changes in the strength of the AMOC influence the position of the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) and thus tropical precipitation 13 . The weakening of the AMOC during HS1 and the Younger Dryas (YD; 12.8-11.8 ka) reduced northward oceanic heat transport across the equator, resulting in a cooler North Atlantic and a warmer South Atlantic, characteristic of a bipolar seesaw response 13,14 . These weakened AMOC phases coincide with enhanced precipitation over northeastern Brazil, whereas strengthened AMOC events, such as the Bølling-Allerød (BA), are associated with reduced rainfall (e.g., Refs. 13,15). While shifts in AMOC and tropical South American precipitation have shown alignment on millennial timescales, there is no clear evidence for such coupling at sub-millennial resolution. High-resolution speleothem δ 18 O records from northeastern Brazil reveal significant centennial-scale variability in precipitation 12 . However, the absence of comparably resolved AMOC records hinders our ability to evaluate the impact of AMOC on these fluctuations. Adding to the ambiguity, 231 Pa/ 230 Th records 3,16 , a proxy for the rate of deepwater transport and thus AMOC strength, show no significant variability within HS1, likely due to the low temporal resolution that hampers the detection of short-term fluctuations. This underscores the pressing need to develop high-resolution AMOC reconstructions to elucidate its role in driving tropical climate shifts on centennial timescales.

The overall rise in atmospheric CO2 during the last deglaciation has been largely attributed to westerly wind-driven upwelling in the Southern Ocean 17 . Notably, increases in atmospheric CO2 18 align with periods of intensified upwelling during HS1 and the YD 17 . Weakened biological and solubility pumps likely contributed to the observed increase in atmospheric CO2 19,20 . However, explaining sub-millennial scale fluctuations in atmospheric CO2 remains challenging 18 . For instance, despite pronounced upwelling during late HS1 17 , a corresponding atmospheric CO2 increase was not observed 18 . To account for certain sub-millennial to centennial-scale changes in atmospheric CO2, the role of the AMOC, which facilitates the transport of carbonrich waters from mid-depth (1000-2500 m) and deep (>2500 m) layers of the Atlantic Ocean to the Southern Ocean, has been considered crucial 21 . Notably, during centennial-scale atmospheric CO2 increases (10-15ppmv over ~200years) at ~14.8 and ~11.7 ka, which are concurrent with periods of AMOC intensification after millennial scale slowdowns, it has been proposed that substantial amounts of CO2 were flushed into the Southern Ocean by a strengthened AMOC and subsequently released to the atmosphere 18,21 . Notably, during these periods of intensified AMOC, strengthened biological and solubility pumps cannot be responsible for CO2 release to the atmosphere 19,20 as they act to drawdown atmospheric CO2, further highlighting the dominant role of AMOC-driven flushing of stored carbon. A centennial-scale atmospheric CO2 increase event at ~16.3 ka, however, remains difficult to explain 18,22 . Alternative mechanisms have been proposed, i.e., outgassing of CO2 led by a shift in the position of the Southern Hemisphere westerlies 18,23 , changes in water mass boundary conditions 21,24 , deepening of the mixed layer and thus an increase in ventilation in the Southern Ocean 22 , or leakage of the marine biological carbon pump 19 . These hypotheses are based on the assumption that no AMOC intensification occurred at ~16.3 ka 18,22 , in contrast to the AMOC intensification events at ~14.8 and ~11.7 ka. However, the absence of highresolution AMOC records for this interval (encompassing HS1) leaves this assumption unverified.

Here we reconstruct benthic-planktic (B-P) radiocarbon ventilation ages from a sediment core collected from the mid-depth western equatorial Atlantic (1.63°S, 43.02°W; 1367m water depth; see Methods Sect. for sediment core details) for the last deglaciation. In addition, results from an Earth system model of intermediate complexity are used as an independent benchmark to assess whether B-P

ventilation ages from our core site serve as a robust indicator of AMOC variability. Further, we utilise the downcore ventilation age record with an unprecedented temporal resolution of 150 years between adjacent samples to reconstruct the centennial-scale variability of the AMOC during HS1 and examine its impact on tropical climate variability and atmospheric CO2 changes.

Results and discussion

Radiocarbon ventilation age record and its reliability as an AMOC proxy

Our B-P radiocarbon ventilation ages (see Methods Sect. for definition) reveal major changes in the ventilation of the mid-depth western equatorial Atlantic (Figs. 1 and 2a). Significantly lower B-P ventilation ages characterise the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) with an average value of 325years (Figs. 1b and 2a). At the LGM-HS1 boundary, we observe a sharp rise in B-P ventilation ages reaching up to 960 years during HS1 (Fig. 2a). Overall elevated ventilation age values are observed within HS1 (average of 695 years), except for the period between 16.5 and 15.8 ka, as well as the period at ~15.4 ka when marked drops in ventilation ages are observed (Fig. 2a). The B-P values are low during the Bølling-Allerød (BA) with an average of 396years (Figs. 1b and 2a). The values drop significantly at the beginning (200years at 14.8 ka) and end (230years at 13 ka) of the BA, while during the mid-BA we observe slightly elevated values (600years at 13.6 ka; Figs. 1b and 2a). The B-P values show an increasing trend within the YD with an average value of 520 years (Figs. 1b and 2a). After rising up to 730years at 11.4 ka, B-P radiocarbon ventilation ages show a sudden decline and remain low throughout the Holocene (average value of 429 years) (Figs. 1b and 2a).

The model output indicates considerable ventilation changes in the Atlantic Ocean since the LGM (Figs. 2b and 3). Please note that the PaleoMIST ice sheet reconstruction 25 has a time step of 2500 years and the model considers interpolated values in between. Thus, the model output in our study (because of the temporal resolution of the prescribed ice-sheet forcing) is not expected to simulate variability within HS1. Instead, the model outputs are strictly used to understand the relationship between AMOC and B-P ventilation age at our study location, while the proxy results are used to understand centennial-scale changes. In agreement with our reconstructed B-P radiocarbon ventilation ages, our modelled radiocarbon ventilation ages show high values during HS1, and low values during the LGM, BA, and Holocene (Figs. 2b and 3). The modelled AMOC strength shows an inverse relationship with the ventilation age values (Figs. 2b and 3), indicating that periods of high ventilation age coincide with periods of weaker AMOC (e.g., HS1) and vice versa (e.g., LGM, BA and Holocene). This relationship is also marked by a strong negative correlation (R 2 = 0.58; p < 0.01) between AMOC strength and B-P ventilation age (Supplementary Fig. 5). The stronger correlation observed when AMOC is below 10Sv suggests that the B-P ventilation age at our site is particularly sensitive to AMOC under weak circulation conditions, i.e., HS1 (Supplementary Fig. 5). In Fig. 3, the close agreement between BPm (modelled B-P ventilation ages) and BPr (reconstructed B-P ventilation ages) during HS1 emphasizes that B-P ventilation ages at our site are more reliably captured during this interval. The absence of a strong relationship during periods of strong AMOC at our study location likely results from (i) variability in planktic values that is independent of AMOC strength, and (ii) similar benthic and planktic values caused by the proximity of our site to the formation sites of northern component water in the North Atlantic (resulting in high relative uncertainties). Please note that the lack of a linear relationship between B-P ventilation age and AMOC strength under strong AMOC conditions is a site-specific artifact, and a stronger relationship is expected at deeper and more distal locations.

Considering the surface ocean as a reference, the bottom water radiocarbon signal (thus the B-P ventilation age) at the study site (middepth western equatorial Atlantic) may depend upon (i) the initial radiocarbon concentration of the bottom water mass at its formation site, (ii) the time taken for the bottom water mass to reach the study site, and (iii) mixing between different water masses with varying radiocarbon signals (e.g., Refs. 26,27). A few other factors can also modify B-P ventilation ages, i.e., bioturbation and influence of terrestrial carbon (e.g., Ref. 28). In our record, the highest B-P ventilation age is observed during HS1 (Fig. 2a). This is similar to B-P ventilation age records from the North Atlantic while the records from the Southern Ocean show the opposite trend with the highest values occurring during the LGM 21,26,27 (Fig. 2g). On millennial timescales, notable similarities between our B-P ventilation age record and 231 Pa/ 230 Th records from the North Atlantic (Refs. 3,16; Fig. 2a, h) suggest that the rate of NADW formation and lateral transport to our study site was the primary factor controlling B-P ventilation age variations. In our ventilation age record, the influence of the other factors listed above seems to be minimal (see Supplementary note 2 for a detailed explanation). The results from our model simulation (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 5) indicate that AMOC strength was the primary factor driving changes in B-P ventilation ages, and that the B-P ventilation age at our site responds most strongly during periods of reduced AMOC.

Changes in AMOC strength and geometry since the LGM

During the LGM our data show relatively low B-P (~325 years) ventilation ages (Fig. 2a). The LGM B-P ventilation ages are comparable to modern values (~350years) indicating that the mid-depth western equatorial Atlantic experienced similar to modern ventilation conditions. The B-P ventilation ages from our model outputs (Fig. 3) also suggest that the Atlantic Ocean mid-depths exhibited similar to modern ventilation during the LGM. Other reconstructions based on radiocarbon ventilation ages from the western equatorial Atlantic found well-ventilated waters at a depth of ~2.4km during the LGM, while deeper depths (~3.5 km) were filled with older/relatively isolated waters 29 . The shallower and vigorously ventilated waters probably had a northern source and spread as far south as 30°S during LGM 30 , and played a key role in the effective ventilation of the mid-depths of the western equatorial Atlantic, while deeper depths (i.e., >2.5km) remained isolated. At the LGM-HS1 boundary, we observe a drastic change in B-P ventilation ages, rapidly changing from 210 years at 18.3 ka to 880years at 17.6 ka (Fig. 2a). A similar sudden change is also observed at a 2.4 km water depth 29 , suggesting that the LGM to HS1 transition in ventilation of the western equatorial Atlantic mid-depths (and thus the change in AMOC strength) was rapid.

Elevated B-P ventilation ages during HS1 are observed in the western equatorial Atlantic mid-depth during HS1 with B-P ventilation ages ~600 years higher than the modern value 31 (Figs. 1 and 2a). These increased B-P ventilation ages during HS1 are coeval with a drop in benthic foraminifera δ 13 C and an increase in 231 Pa/ 230 Th from the same sediment core, i.e., GeoB16206-1 (Ref. 32; Supplementary Fig. 6). During HS1, significant changes in NADW formation have been noted with intense iceberg discharge 5,33 that led to surface water freshening in the high latitudes of the North Atlantic 34 . In response to surface salinity perturbations, NADW formation experienced a significant slowdown 3 . The North Atlantic was occupied by old waters with B-P ventilation ages rising up to ~2000 years at depths below 2 km 29 . In the western equatorial Atlantic, B-P ventilation ages reached values of ~2700years at ~2.4km water depth 29 . The Southern Ocean was also occupied by old waters during this period with B-P ventilation ages of ~2000 years 27 . Overall, the mid and deeper depths (>1 km) of the Atlantic Ocean were filled with exceptionally old and isolated waters during HS1 compared to modern time. Such high B-P ventilation ages during HS1 point to a drastic slowdown of NADW formation and thus a markedly weak AMOC (e.g., Refs. 3,35). Accordingly, our simulated weak AMOC (Fig. 3c) during HS1, supports earlier conjectures that NADW formation experienced a significant slowdown during this period (e.g., Refs. 3,16). On the contrary, deeper depths (i.e., >2.5 km) of the equatorial Atlantic show relatively lower B-P ventilation ages compared to mid-depths as recorded in a sedimentary record (Ref. 29) due to the influence of better-ventilated southern sourced waters.

Thus, we use the high-temporal resolution of our B-P ventilation age record between ~18 and 13 ka (covering HS1 and the BA) to understand shortlived (i.e., centennial-scale) changes in AMOC strength and its impacts on tropical climate. The high sedimentation rate during this time period in our record (>50cm/ka) ensures the minimum effect of bioturbation 37 on the radiocarbon signal (Supplementary Fig. 6g). One of the most striking features of our record is the pronounced double plunge structure within HS1 (Fig. 4a). Importantly, a similar structure has also been observed in high-temporal resolution tropical South American hydroclimate records 10,12 . HS1 is subdivided into two intervals (Fig. 4a): an early phase (17.8 to 16.5 ka) marked by a relatively stable but weak AMOC, and a late phase (16.5 to 14.8 ka) characterized by a weak yet variable AMOC. Notably, the late HS1 features the double plunge structure marked by two prominent centennial-scale AMOC intensification events centred around 16.3 and 15.4 ka (Fig. 4a).

We observe high ventilation ages between 17.6 and 16.5 ka, indicating a weakened AMOC (Fig. 4a). This period coincides with the first occurrence of ice-rafted debris in North Atlantic sediment cores during HS1 38 , as well as with an increase in magnetic susceptibility in the Iberian margin 39 indicating a higher concentration of coarse detrital magnetite, often associated with ice-rafted debris, likely due to enhanced iceberg discharge 40 (Fig. 4d). In tropical South America, this period is marked by negative anomalies in speleothem δ 18 O from northeastern and central-western Brazil 12,41 , suggesting increased precipitation (Fig. 4b, c). High planktic foraminifera Ba/Ca ratios recorded off northernmost South America during the same period resulted from increased northeastern Brazil riverine discharge 10 . Evidence for increased precipitation during this period also comes from sediment cores retrieved off northeastern Brazil 12,42 . Previously published geochemical and luminescence sensitivity records obtained from our sediment core 43 indicate that riverine discharge from northeastern Brazil and, thus, precipitation, also increased during early HS1.

Increased precipitation over these Brazilian regions can be linked to a weakening of the AMOC strength and consequent decreased northward oceanic heat transport during early HS1 44 , shifting southwards the position of the thermal equator and ultimately the ITCZ 13 .

The period between 16.5 and 15.8 ka is represented by a sudden drop (reaching 450years) and a subsequent rise in ventilation age values (Fig. 4a). These values are only slightly higher than the values of the BA (average 396years) and the Holocene (average 429years), indicating a major centennial-scale strengthening of the AMOC within HS1. After reaching 450 years at 16.3 ka, ventilation ages increase again, reaching 710 years at 15.8 ka, indicating a recovery of AMOC strength for a short period (Fig. 4a). A recent modelling study indicates that when the AMOC operates in a weak background state close to convective instability, relatively small perturbations in surface buoyancy forcing can trigger abrupt transitions (at least in the CLIMBER-X model) toward a stronger overturning mode 45 . This mechanism provides a physically plausible explanation for the rapid strengthening of the AMOC inferred from our ventilation-age record during mid-HS1. A few high temporal resolution records show that this period was a phase of sudden hydroclimatic shifts over tropical South America 10,12 (Fig. 4b, c). For example, the δ 18 O record from a highly resolved and absolute dated speleothem from northeastern Brazil suggests an intermittent period of dryness from 16.66 to 16.11 ka 12 . Concurrent with the speleothem record, two proxy records tracking the terrigenous sediment discharge from northeastern Brazil into the western equatorial Atlantic also suggest a similar dry period 12 . The intensification of AMOC during this period enhanced northward oceanic heat transport 44 , which shifted the thermal equator and consequently the ITCZ northward, leading to reduced precipitation over northeastern Brazil 13 . A sea surface temperature reconstruction from the subtropical eastern North Atlantic suggests a warm excursion of up to 3 °C during mid-HS1 within an overall cold HS1 46 , confirming the re-establishment of the northward oceanic heat transport during this period.

The period between 15.8 and 14.7 ka is again characterized by overall high ventilation ages that are similar to early HS1, suggesting another weak AMOC period within HS1 (Fig. 4a). This time window roughly aligns with the second massive occurrence of ice-rafted debris identified in sediment cores from the high latitudes of the North Atlantic during HS1 38 . The weak AMOC forced a renewed accumulation of heat in the South Atlantic, which resulted in an increase in precipitation over northeastern Brazil 12 , similarly to early HS1. An additional noteworthy feature observed during late HS1 in our record is a sudden drop in ventilation ages at 15.4 ka, which may indicate another centennial-scale episode of an AMOC intensification (Fig. 4a). This period is concurrent with an episode of short-term precipitation decrease in northeastern Brazil 12 (Fig. 4b), possibly as a result of northward shifted ITCZ. Ventilation of western equatorial Atlantic mid-depths and its impact on atmospheric CO2

Atmospheric CO2 concentration increased by ~80 ppmv during the last deglaciation 18,47 (Fig. 2d). Considering that the terrestrial biosphere acted as an overall sink of CO2 48 , leakage of substantial amounts of CO2 from the abyssal ocean into the atmosphere is considered the best possible explanation for the deglacial rise in atmospheric CO2 27,48 . Various oceanic processes 17,20,21,27 influenced the overall release of CO2 from the Southern Ocean. For example, flushing 21,27 (lateral advection of CO2 associated with southward-flowing water masses that supplied CO2 from the deep and mid-depth Atlantic to the Southern Ocean), and upwelling 17 (that supplied the CO2 rich waters from the deep Southern Ocean to the surface) were responsible for making CO2 rich waters available at the surface of Southern Ocean where air-sea gas exchange ultimately released the CO2 to the atmosphere. In addition, reduced biological 19 and/or solubility 20 pumps also assisted in the release of excess CO2 from the surface ocean to the atmosphere. Notably, the flushing and upwelling processes operated under different millennial-scale AMOC states, i.e., lateral flushing of CO2-rich waters occurred during periods of enhanced AMOC (such as the BA), whereas strong Southern Ocean upwelling was concurrent with weakened AMOC (as generally seen during HS1 and the YD) 17,29,49 . Importantly, an intensification of the AMOC is not necessarily linked to enhanced upwelling in the Southern Ocean, since today much of the North Atlantic Deep Water formation is balanced to a large extent by upward diapycnal diffusion in the Pacific and Indian Oceans 50 . The ventilation age record from our study site (Fig. 2a) indicates that the mid-depths of the western equatorial Atlantic played a crucial role in temporarily storing CO2 during HS1 and the YD, while this stored CO2 was later transported to the Southern Ocean during the BA, and the beginning of the Holocene. Overall, the Southern Ocean served as a crucial conduit between the ocean and atmosphere, with upwelling driven by westerly winds playing a direct role in regulating atmospheric CO2 levels 17,23,27 .

Along with the millennial-scale periods (i.e., HS1, YD) of gradual CO2 rise observed during the last deglaciation, a few short-term centennial-scale steps of sudden CO2 rise 18 have been noted. Among them, the events at ~16.3, 14.8, and 11.7 ka are marked by significant atmospheric CO2 rises (10-15 ppmv increase in atmospheric CO2 in ~200years) 18 . Additionally, several minor CO2 rise events are also observed, which are characterized by a CO2 increase exceeding 5 ppmv within a 100years span, one notable instance occurring at ~15.4 ka 18 . Two of these short-term steps at ~14.8 ka (HS1-BA boundary) and ~11.7 ka (YD-Holocene boundary) are concurrent with the abrupt strengthening of the AMOC. These two centennial-scale events are marked by sudden drops in ventilation ages in our records (Figs. 2a and 4a) and swift temperature increases over Greenland 9 (Fig. 2f). The simultaneous occurrences of AMOC resumptions and increases in atmospheric CO2 suggest that the atmospheric CO2 steps at ~14.8 and 11.7 ka were associated with AMOC intensifications 21,51 .

Our record also indicates temporary intensifications of the AMOC within HS1 at 16.5-15.8 ka and at ~15.4 ka (Fig. 4a). We suggest that a strengthened AMOC during these events resulted in flushing of a significant amount of CO2 from the Atlantic mid-depths to the Southern Ocean 21 . With the intensification of the AMOC, westerly winds over the Southern Ocean probably migrated northward 17 , potentially reducing upwelling. However, despite the centennial-scale periods of intensified AMOC during the last deglaciation, the Southern Ocean still experienced sufficient upwelling during late HS1 17 , which transported the flushed CO2 to the surface ocean. A recent study by Ref. 20 suggests that the solubility pump played a dominant role in releasing CO2 from the surface Southern Ocean to the atmosphere during HS1. However, the two centennial-scale AMOC intensification events within HS1 would have increased interhemispheric heat transport, leading to a decline in SST in the Southern Ocean 13 . A decrease in SST is expected to have strengthened the solubility pump 20,52 , thereby limiting its role as the primary driver of CO2 release from the Southern Ocean. On top of it, enhanced ventilation facilitated the upward transport of nutrientrich waters in the North Atlantic, strengthening the biological pump, also limiting CO2 release from the North Atlantic 19,52 . Ref. 53 suggests that the tropical terrestrial carbon reservoir functioned as a CO2 source to the atmosphere during periods of southward ITCZ migration. On the contrary, during AMOC intensification events, like those at 16.5-15.8 and ~15.4 ka, when the ITCZ shifted northward, this reservoir likely did not contribute as a major source. Thus, we suggest that lateral flushing of CO2-rich waters from the Atlantic mid-depths significantly contributed to the ~20 ppmv atmospheric CO2 rise at ~16.3 ka and to the ~5 ppmv atmospheric CO2 rise at ~15.4 ka (Fig. 4a). Indeed, carbon was generally stored in the Atlantic mid-depths during HS1 as documented in low resolution pH sensitive proxies 54 and may have been further enhanced by the Pacific-Atlantic carbon sea saw processes that facilitated inter-basin carbon transfer 55,56 .

Our refined understanding allows us to reassess submillennialscale atmospheric CO2 fluctuations during HS1. Between 17.5 ka (~196 ppmv) and 16.7 ka (~217 ppmv) 18 , a steady increase in atmospheric CO2 is observed (Fig. 4g), primarily driven by Southern Ocean upwelling, supported by weakened biological and solubility pumps 19,20 . After that, despite substantial upwelling 17 , atmospheric CO2 temporarily declines between 16.7 (~217 ppmv) and 16.5 ka (~212 ppmv) 18 , suggesting a scenario in which the Southern Ocean no longer held excess CO2 to be released to the atmosphere. A rapid rise in atmospheric CO2 is observed between 16.5 (~212 ppmv) and 16.2 ka (~231 ppmv) 18 . The initial increase in this atmospheric CO2 rise aligns with the onset of the ventilation age drop in our record (Fig. 4a, g). This indicates an AMOC driven rapid transfer of CO2-rich waters towards the Southern Ocean and its quick release to the atmosphere. The rise in atmospheric CO2 halts as ventilation age begins to increase (Fig. 4a), indicating a cutoff in the CO2 supply at that point in time. Between 16.2 ka (~231 ppmv) and 15.5 ka (~227 ppmv), atmospheric CO2 remained relatively stable 18 . Around 15.4 ka, another influx of CO2-rich waters was delivered to the Southern Ocean via an enhanced AMOC, leading to a ~5 ppmv atmospheric CO2 rise. Again, from 15.3 ka (~233 ppmv) to 14.8 ka (~232 ppmv), atmospheric CO2 remained relatively constant. We propose that AMOC-driven CO2 flushing plays a crucial role in explaining centennial-scale atmospheric CO2 increases.

In summary, this study uses high-resolution radiocarbon ventilation ages from a sediment core in the western equatorial Atlantic to reconstruct the variability of the AMOC strength during the last deglaciation, particularly focusing on HS1. The findings of this study are essential for comprehending centennial-scale variations in the strength of the AMOC and their effects on global climate dynamics through disruptions in interhemispheric heat transport and release of oceanic CO2. Within an overall weakened AMOC state during HS1, our record reveals two centennial-scale AMOC intensification events. Our study shows that the state of a weakened AMOC during HS1 is less stable than previously thought 57 . The period between 16.5 and 15.8 ka, as well as at ~15.4 ka, were marked by a strengthened AMOC, resulting in increased interhemispheric heat transport. A shift in interhemispheric heat transport led to an alteration in the mean position of the ITCZ, which consequently affected precipitation patterns over tropical South America 12,13 . We propose that episodes of AMOC intensification transported substantial volumes of CO2-rich water from the mid-depth Atlantic to the Southern Ocean, where it was rapidly outgassed to the atmosphere. This provides a challenge for fully coupled climatecarbon cycle models to be applied for the centennial events during the last deglaciation and possible future changes in AMOC 2 .

Methods

Marine sediment core

The investigated sediment core (i.e., GeoB16206-1) was raised from the western equatorial Atlantic (1.63°S, 43.02°W) from a water depth of 1367m during RV Maria S. Merian research cruise MSM20/3 (Ref. 58; Supplementary Fig. 1). At present, the equatorial Atlantic is bathed by northern-sourced North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW) between 1200 and 4000 m water depth and southern-sourced Antarctic Intermediate Water (AAIW) and Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW) above and below NADW, respectively (Ref. 59). Thus, bottom water at the studied site is currently dominated by northern-sourced NADW and Nd isotopic data from a nearby sediment core at a similar depth suggests that similar conditions prevailed throughout the last 26 ka 16 .

Radiocarbon analyses and chronology of the sediment core

Radiocarbon analyses on both surface-dwelling planktic (Globigerinoides ruber and Globigerinoides sacculifer) and benthic (mixed epifaunal species; excluding agglutinated specimens) foraminifera were performed in the Poznan Radiocarbon Laboratory, Poland (see Supplementary note 1). Foraminifera specimens were preferentially picked from >250μm size fraction. However, in the absence of enough material, the 125-250 μm size fraction was also considered. A total of 43 samples were analysed for planktic and benthic foraminifera, totalling 86 radiocarbon analyses, out of which 12 planktic ages were reported earlier 60 .

A multistep procedure (adapted from Ref. 29) was followed to build the chronology of sediment core GeoB16206-1 using both stratigraphic tie points and radiocarbon ages of planktic foraminifera with varying reservoir ages (Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Figs. 2-4). First, we determined stratigraphic ages for core GeoB162061 by aligning the bulk sediment ln(Fe/Ca) data from that core with the high-resolution NGRIP dust record 61 on the GICC05 timescale 62 (Supplementary Fig. 2). A comparison of western equatorial Atlantic and Greenland records is supported by the marked coupling between climatic fluctuations over Greenland and precipitation over Brazil 63 . The estimated stratigraphic ages were considered calendar ages and were interpolated to the depths where radiocarbon ages are available. Second, planktic foraminifera radiocarbon ages were used to estimate past local surface reservoir ages using the R package ResAge 64 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Local surface reservoir age offsets (ΔR) were calculated by taking the difference between the reservoir age values and the modern global mean surface reservoir age (~550 years) 65 . Third, the planktic radiocarbon ages were calibrated for past 14 C production changes with the Marine20 calibration curve that already includes a mean surface reservoir age 65 , and corrected with the calculated ΔR. A Bayesian approach using the RBacon package 66 was used to build the age-depth model of core GeoB16206-1 combining both stratigraphic tie points and planktic foraminifera radiocarbon ages (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Ventilation age estimation

Ventilation ages are reported as the offset of bottom water radiocarbon ages obtained from benthic foraminifera (B) with reference to either (i) surface water radiocarbon age obtained from shallowdwelling planktic foraminifera (P) thus B-P, or (ii) the calendar age i.e., atmospheric reservoir (Atm) thus B-Atm 67,68 . The B-P method (difference between co-deposited planktic and benthic specimens of foraminifera) does not require the knowledge of calendar age and does not take into consideration variable reservoir effects in shallow and deep marine water masses 68 . However, the B-Atm metrics compare the deep water with the atmosphere rather than the surface water, and thus the adopted age-depth model becomes crucial 67 . The difference between B-P derived and B-Atm derived ventilation ages is also a product of the changes in surface reservoir age. In this study, our paleoceanographic interpretations primarily rely on B-P estimates.

Marine radiocarbon simulation

We apply the Earth system model of intermediate complexity CLIMBER-X 69 , version 3, which we have equipped with radiocarbon and inert gas tracers and run over the past 70 ka. The CLIMBER-X version 3 70 includes an updated bottom-water friction parameterization linked to topographic drag, differing from the version 2 71 . This updated parametrization has been used in various recent studies such as analysis of millennial-scale AMOC variations carried out by Ref. 45. In the present study, the model is forced with insolation 72 , greenhouse gas concentrations (CO2, CH4 and N2O) 47 , continental ice sheets and their meltwater discharge according to the PaleoMIST reconstruction 25 (for their implementation in CLIMBER-X see Ref. 73), and atmospheric Δ 14 C (Δ 14 Catm) 74 . The horizontal model resolution in atmosphere and ocean is 5° × 5° each. Vertical resolution in the ocean is 23 layers of uneven thickness. Sea level variations are taken into account, and the model includes a flexible land-sea mask for this purpose 69 . Radiocarbon is implemented in terms of Δ 14 C, that is, the normalized 14 C/ 12 C ratio corrected for carbon-isotopic fractionation. Following Ref. 75, we only consider the Δ 14 C of dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC, Δ 14 CDIC) and neglect biological sources and sinks of marine 14 C. Thus, Δ 14 CDIC is simulated as a single prognostic tracer that is conservative except for its radioactive decay and is only linked to the carbon cycle via the timescale of 14 CO2 exchange between the atmosphere and the ocean. In our model, this timescale depends on the prescribed partial pressure of atmospheric CO2 47 (assuming local solubility equilibrium at the sea 76,77 ) as well as on the internally simulated windspeeds (parameterized according to Ref. 78) and sea ice concentrations. The relative uncertainty between this simplified and more comprehensive Δ 14 CDIC modelling approaches is smaller than 5% (Ref. 79).

Our simulation starts at 70 ka from a pre-industrial initial state that was determined in a previous 20 ka spin-up integration with steady climate forcing for 1850 CE and Δ 14 Catm=0‰. Afterwards, the model is run forward in time until 1950 CE (0.000 ka) with transient climate forcing and Δ 14 Catm values, except for 70-55 ka (the period prior to the IntCal20 reconstruction 74 ) when Δ 14 Catm=528‰. At the end of the simulation, Δ 14 CDIC values are converted to B-Atm ages (in 14 C years) with respect to contemporaneous Δ 14 Catm, and to B-P ventilation ages, subtracting the B-Atm ages of the surface layer from the corresponding B-Atm ages at deeper layers. Our simulation coherently captures the distribution of B-Atm and B-P ventilation ages in the western Atlantic reconstructed for the late Holocene 80,81 albeit with a positive bias in the upper water column (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8).

In this study, we only consider the ventilation ages calculated at the model grid coordinates nearest to the sediment core site (42.5°W, 2.5°S at 5 m and ~1450 m depth). A comparison between model forcing parameters, AMOC strength, and B-P ventilation age is shown in Supplementary Fig. 9.
