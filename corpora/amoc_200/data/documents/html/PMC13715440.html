EARTH SCIENCES

Special Topic: Complex Marine Ecosystems and Planetary Boundary

Tipping points and cascading transitions: methods, principles, and evidence

Sheng Fang 1 , Ziyan Wang 1 , Jürgen Kurths 2 , 3 , ∗ and Jingfang Fan 1 , 2 , ∗

ABSTRACT

This review synthesizes recent advancements in understanding tipping points and cascading transitions within the Earth system, framing them through the lens of nonlinear dynamics and complexity science. It outlines the fundamental concepts of tipping elements, large-scale subsystems like the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation and the Amazon rainforest, and classifies tipping mechanisms into bifurcation-, noise-, and rate-induced types. The article critically evaluates methods for detecting early-warning signals, particularly those based on critical slowing down, while also acknowledging their limitations and the promise of non-conventional indicators. Furthermore, we explore the significant risk of cascading transitions between interacting tipping elements, often modeled using conceptual network models. This shows that such interactions can substantially increase systemic risk under global warming. The review concludes by outlining key challenges related to data limitations and methodological robustness, and emphasizes the promising role of artificial intelligence and complex network science in advancing prediction and risk assessment of Earth system tipping dynamics.

Keywords: tipping point, tipping cascade, bifurcation, early-warning signals, complex network

INTRODUCTION

The ocean, covering about 71% of Earth's surface, is a cornerstone of the Earth system, underpinning global climate regulation, sustaining marine biodiversity, and supporting socioeconomic development. It has absorbed nearly 93% of the excess heat and about 30% of anthropogenic CO 2 emissions [1 ,2 ], harbors ∼2.2 million species [3 ], and contributes around USD 2.6 trillion annually to the global economy [4 ]. However, under the combined pressures of global warming and human activities, the ocean is experiencing profound and systemic changes, including sealevel rise, warming, acidification, and deoxygenation [5 ]. These interconnected processes are eroding marine ecosystem resilience and may push subsystems toward irreversible regime shifts. This threat is especially acute in coastal regions owing to their position at the land-ocean boundary and heightened sensitivity to environmental changes. Since they are exposed simultaneously to terrestrial, marine, and human-induced disturbances, coastal systems often face amplified cumulative impacts that can accelerate ecological degradation and push them closer to critical thresholds. For example, global warming and anthropogenic nutrient inputs have caused severe deoxygenation in the Baltic Sea [6 ], which reduces fishery yields and promotes harmful algal blooms, which are extremely difficult to reverse through governance or intervention.

The marine environment exemplifies a complex adaptive system, in which physical, biological, and socioeconomic components are dynamically coupled through nonlinear feedbacks operating across multiple spatial and temporal scales. Understanding such intertwined processes requires a holistic framework, as provided by Earth System Science (ESS). ESS conceptualizes the planet as an integrated system of interacting physical, chemical, biological, and anthropogenic subsystems [7 ]. It seeks to reveal how natural processes and human activities jointly shape global dynamics, encompassing the climate system, biogeochemical cycles, and ecosystems. By integrating insights from geology, meteorology, oceanography, ecology, and the social sciences, ESS provides a comprehensive framework for addressing grand challenges in sustainability, resilience, and planetary boundaries, and for guiding the informed management of Earth's complex systems.

Within the ESS framework, the concept of tipping points has attracted increasing attention [8 ,9 ]. A tipping point refers to a critical threshold beyond which small perturbations can trigger large and often irreversible changes in a system's state. The corresponding large-scale subsystems of the Earth, which are known as tipping elements , include the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), the Amazon rainforest, the Greenland Ice Sheet (GIS), and the West Antarctic Ice Sheet (WAIS), etc. Various methods, including earlywarning signals (EWS), have been designed to anticipate the tipping points [10 ]. Importantly, these elements are not independent; their interactions can induce cascading effects that amplify systemic risk [11 ]. For instance, rapid Arctic sea-ice loss introduces freshwater into the North Atlantic, weakening the AMOC. This slowdown can reduce rainfall over South America, accelerating the Amazon forest dieback. The resulting carbon release enhances global warming, potentially destabilizing the West Antarctic Ice Sheet [9 ,12 ]. Recent assessments suggest that several major tipping elements are approaching, or may already have crossed, their critical thresholds [9 ], and that exceeding 1.5°C of global warming could trigger multiple, interconnected tipping events [13 ]. Recent evidence indicates that warm-water coral reefs are already confronting unprecedented mortality, suggesting that these ecosystems may have crossed critical thermal thresholds [14 ].

In this review, we provide an integrative synthesis that connects diverse tipping mechanisms within a comprehensive framework. We systematically bridge the theoretical foundations of tipping dynamics with state-of-the-art detection methodologies, explicitly expanding beyond classical temporal and spatial EWS to encompass advanced multivariate indicators. Crucially, we extend this framework to interconnected systems by highlighting recent, timely developments in cascading transitions, including overshoot-induced cascade risks, indirect tipping pathways, and nonlocal interactions. Finally, we critically examine emerging frontiers, emphasizing how explainable and physics-informed artificial intelligence, together with complex network science, can overcome traditional methodological limitations. Ultimately, our contribution lies in weaving these rapidly evolving strands of research into a cohesive paradigm, thereby advancing the prediction, understanding, and risk assessment of systemic transitions in the interconnected Earth system.

TIPPING POINT AND TIPPING ELEMENT

The concept of "tipping point" originated from observations of abrupt sociological changes, capturing how small perturbations can lead to disproportionately large outcomes [15 ]. Over time, the term gained traction beyond the social sciences and has been increasingly adopted across diverse disciplines [10 ], including ESS. A prominent application arises in climate science, where such thresholds are closely linked to what was previously termed abrupt climate change [16 ].

Since the 1970s, the understanding of abrupt climate change has evolved from a theoretical notion to an empirically grounded paradigm. This shift was largely driven by high-resolution paleoclimatic archives, which revealed that Quaternary climate history was punctuated by rapid, highmagnitude oscillations, such as the Younger Dryas stadial [17 ]. Broecker [18 ] played a pioneering role in advancing the hypothesis that the climate system exhibits nonlinear behavior capable of rapid reorganization, particularly through perturbations to the AMOC. Subsequent syntheses of ice-core and marine sediment records further reinforced this view, demonstrating the recurrent nature of abrupt transitions throughout Earth's history [16 ].

Building upon this foundation, Lenton et al. [8 ] formally defined a tipping point as the critical threshold of a control parameter, ρc , beyond which an infinitesimal perturbation induces a significant change, ˆ F , in the system state F within a finite observation time T . They further introduced the concept of climate tipping elements , referring to subcontinental-scale components of the Earth system that may cross a tipping point and transition into a qualitatively new state under the influence of climate change or anthropogenic forcing. These tipping elements are of particular policy relevance, as their activation could amplify global temperature rise and destabilize other parts of the system. Moreover, these elements are not independent, where transitions in one tipping element can trigger cascading effects across others, potentially leading to widespread and irreversible transformations of the Earth system.

A typical example of a tipping element is the Amazon rainforest, which harbors over 10% of Earth's terrestrial biodiversity and serves as one of the planet's largest carbon sinks, absorbing vast amounts of atmospheric carbon dioxide through photosynthesis [19 ]. However, under the combined pressures of climate change and intensifying human activities, extensive regions of the Amazon rainforest are projected to undergo large-scale degradation and dieback in the coming decades [20 ]. A recent study [21 ] suggests that by 2050, ∼10% -47% of the Amazon rainforest could be exposed to compounding disturbances, potentially triggering abrupt ecosystem transitions and amplifying regional climate change.

Faced with such grave risks from negative tipping elements, a promising avenue for climate mitigation lies in harnessing positive tipping points (PTPs), which deliberate socio-technical shifts where targeted interventions initiate selfreinforcing feedbacks that accelerate decarbonization [26 ]. Unlike the often irreversible Earth system tipping points, PTPs arise when reinforcing mechanisms, such as economies of scale, learning-by-doing, and social diffusion, overcome the inertia of incumbent high-carbon systems. Empirical examples are emerging: the rapid adoption of electric vehicles in several regions exhibits EWS of an approaching technological transition [27 ]. Moreover, strategic policy interventions, such as mandates and phase-out regulations, can create "super-leverage points," triggering cascading changes across coupled sectors like transport and power [28 ]. Thus, alongside managing risks from climatic tipping elements, proactive efforts to identify and trigger PTPs offer a crucial pathway toward stabilizing the Earth system.

Based on the underlying mechanisms, whether state transitions are triggered by internal dynamics or external perturbations, tipping phenomena can generally be classified into three causally distinct types: (i)

bifurcation-induced tipping (B-tipping), (ii) noiseinduced tipping (N-tipping), and (iii) rate-dependent tipping (R-tipping) [14 ,22 ].

(i) B-tipping occurs when a slow, continuous variation in system parameters (e.g., external forcing or climate trends) drives the system through a bifurcation, causing a stable state, such as a fixed point or a periodic orbit, to lose stability or vanish. This loss of stability leads to an abrupt transition to an alternative attractor. (ii) In contrast, N-tipping arises in multistable systems, where stochastic fluctuations (e.g., interannual variability in the climate system) can push the system across a potential barrier, inducing a noisedriven transition from one stable state to another. (iii) R-tipping occurs when the rate of parameter change is sufficiently rapid that the system cannot adiabatically follow its quasi-static equilibrium. As a result, the system departs from the expected attractor before the bifurcation point is reached, or it may delay the transition, which leads to an early or late tipping event.

Beyond these basic types, more refined frameworks have been proposed to classify tipping transitions, especially when multiple timescales or spatial structures are at play. For instance, Ref. [29 ] categorizes transitions into three types, the state transition, basin transition, and distribution transition, which are based on the interplay among the timescale of parameter variation ( τλ), the timescale of state transition ( τtran ), the ergodic timescale ( τergo ), and the noise intensity. Moreover, when spatial heterogeneity and subsystem couplings are explicitly considered, such as in coastal regions, Ref. Early-warning signals and tipping point detection

Since tipping events can cause profound and often irreversible shifts in system states, detecting and anticipating their onset is of paramount importance. Yet, the intrinsic complexity of empirical systems often makes it difficult to determine the critical threshold in advance. Fortunately, many complex systems exhibit universal signatures, such as rising variance, autocorrelation, and skewness, as they approach critical transitions. These phenomena are collectively referred to as EWS [10 ]. A range of EWS detection methods have been developed and are summarized in Table 2 , following Ref. [31 ]. Among these approaches, one of the most widely applied is based on the concept of critical slowing down (CSD) [32 ], which describes the progressive decline in a system's recovery rate as it nears a bifurcation point in B-tipping scenarios. Indicators derived from CSD, such as variance, temporal and spatial correlations, and recovery rate, are particularly useful for detecting imminent transitions, as summarized in Table 2 .

Another commonly used technique is detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) [35 ], designed to quantify long-term correlations and scaling properties in noisy, nonstationary time series. EWS approaches have been widely implemented in the analysis of diverse climate subsystems [11 ,34 ,39 ]. For instance, using a century-long dataset (1900-2020) of sea surface temperature and salinity from the subpolar North Atlantic, the author applied statistical EWS indicators analysis and presented empirical evidence suggesting that the AMOC may be approaching a tipping point [34 ]. Their analysis revealed a pronounced increase in both autocorrelation and variance over time, hallmarks of CSD that indicate a loss of dynamical resilience. These results suggest that the AMOC has been progressively weakening, particularly since the mid-20th century, and may be nearing a tipping point. While the precise timing of a potential collapse remains uncertain, this study provides the first robust empirical warning that such an event could be imminent, underscoring the urgent need for enhanced observation and proactive climate mitigation efforts.

In addition to temporal indicators, CSD leaves distinct signatures within the spatial patterns of connected subsystems approaching a critical threshold [40 ,41 ]. When a spatially extended system nears a tipping point, its localized recovery rate from external perturbations diminishes, causing neighboring regions to become increasingly synchronized. This spatial manifestation of CSD leads to a marked increase in spatial autocorrelation, meaning that adjacent patches exhibit highly similar states [40 ]. Furthermore, the slowed recovery amplifies spatial fluctuations, resulting in elevated spatial variance and prominent changes in spatial skewness [41 ]. Other spatial metrics, such as an expanding spatial recovery length, have also been identified as robust leading indicators of impending regime shifts [42 ]. Crucially, these spatial EWS offer a significant advantage over their temporal counterparts: they can often be derived from a single or a few high-resolution spatial snapshots, such as those obtained via satellite remote sensing, thereby circumventing the strict requirement for long, uninterrupted historical time series [43 ].

Moreover, these indicators are particularly valuable in large-scale climate and ecological systems characterized by strong spatial organization. Compared to purely temporal approaches, incorporating spatial information enhances predictive capability and improves the robustness of EWS detection, especially when observational records are relatively short or heavily obscured by noise [40 ]. Building on these spatial methodologies, recent advancements have expanded the application of CSD theory to highly complex spatio-temporal domains, including interconnected ecosystems and transient landforms [44 ]. For instance, a novel framework based on eigenvalue tracking and Floquet multipliers has been developed to quantify resilience directly from spatio-temporal data without the prerequisite of removing underlying trends or seasonality. This innovative approach not only successfully recovers spatially explicit destabilization patterns in the Amazon rainforest and predicts glacier surge onsets, but also demonstrates remarkable robustness to the varying noise levels inherently associated with multi-sensor satellite records.

Even though CSD-based EWS have been successfully applied across a wide range of disciplines, they still face notable limitations. These methods are fundamentally grounded in the assumption that perturbations can be linearized near an equilibrium state, an assumption that holds primarily for B-tipping scenarios. However, this premise breaks down in the cases of N-tipping and R-tipping. In N-tipping, stochastic fluctuations exert strong, nonlinear influences that cannot be captured by linearization. In R-tipping, the system trajectory departs from any quasi-static equilibrium due to the rapid variation of external parameters, rendering linearization-based approaches invalid.

To address these shortcomings, several nonCSD-based EWS indicators have been proposed, which do not rely on the approach of the recovery rate toward zero. Instead, they detect other high-order statistical signatures, which emerge as the system nears a tipping point and are particularly suitable for systems exhibiting N-tipping or evolving far from quasi-static equilibrium. For example, skewness [45 ], which quantifies the asymmetry of the system-state distribution, can reveal nonlinear shifts toward a tipping point by identifying increasing occurrences of extreme deviations in one direction. Another notable indicator is flickering [46 ], a phenomenon in which the system intermittently switches between alternative stable states under stochastic forcing. Such behavior reflects the progressive loss of resilience as the system begins to explore adjacent basins of attraction. Together, these non-CSD-based indicators provide valuable diagnostic tools for anticipating critical transitions in systems where traditional CSD-based methods fail to apply.

Another prominent information-theoretic, non-CSD-based indicator is the Fisher information, which quantifies the dynamic order and certainty of a system's state distribution without relying on CSD [47 ,48 ]. In complex multivariate systems, a stable regime is characterized by high dynamic order and bounded variance, yielding a high and steady value of Fisher information [49 ]. However, as the system approaches a critical threshold and its resilience deteriorates, the state trajectories become increasingly erratic and disordered [50 ]. This progressive loss of system organization is reflected as a steady decline in Fisher information, providing a robust EWS of an impending regime shift well before the actual transition occurs [48 ]. Furthermore, Fisher information is highly advantageous because it can effectively collapse complex, multivariate timeseries data into a single systemic index, offering a reliable sentinel for both temporal and spatial transitions in ecological and socio-economic networks [51 ]. CASCADING TRANSITIONS

From a risk-assessment perspective, the catastrophic impact of crossing a tipping point arises not only from the transition of an individual subsystem, but also from its capacity to trigger cascading effects across other tipping elements through complex interactions. Such cascades can propagate through interconnected components of the Earth system, including the cryosphere, biosphere, and ocean circulation, forming feedback loops that amplify the initial perturbation and may lead to large-scale, potentially irreversible consequences. A recent study indicates that exceeding a global warming threshold of 1.5°C could trigger multiple climate tipping points [13 ], and that some critical thresholds might even lie below this level [8 ]. This highlights that the cumulative risk of interacting tipping elements is substantially greater than that inferred from analyzing them in isolation, especially when considering synergistic effects and domino-like cascades of tipping events.

Although the terms "cascade" and "domino effect" are often used interchangeably across disciplines, a key distinction lies in whether causality is a necessary condition for a cascade [33 ,75 ]. Klose et al. [76 ] proposed three distinct patterns of multiple tipping dynamics: the domino cascade , the two-phase cascade , and the joint cascade . To illustrate these, we consider a leading system X1 and a following system X2 , driven by external control parameters r1 and r2 , respectively. Typically, the driver r2 for the following system X2 is set to be a constant.

As r1 changes, the leading system X1 may tip and, through direct coupling, induce tipping in X2 , a process known as a domino cascade . This form emphasizes causality, where tipping signals propagate from the leading to the following system through explicit interaction, and has been widely used to model signal transmission between tipping elements in the Earth system [12 ,77 ]. In contrast, a two-phase cascade describes a situation in which both systems X1 and X2 cross their tipping points sequentially as r1 varies, yet the tipping of X1 does not immediately cause that of X2 ; rather, further sustained change in r1 leads to the subsequent tipping of X2 . Thus, causality is not a necessary condition for this type of cascade. This mechanism has been invoked to explain the consecutive tipping of the AMOC (as X1 ) and the Antarctic Ice Sheet (as X2 ) during the Eocene-Oligocene transition, accounting for the stepwise shifts observed in oxygen isotope records [33 ,78 ]. In a joint cascade , two systems X1 and X2 tip simultaneously, as observed in some spatially extended bistable ecosystems [40 ,79 ].

This distinction is useful because only the first class encodes a direct propagation by construction, while the others can arise from common forcing, timescale separation, or mutual destabilization. Recent work has broadened this taxonomy further. Indirect tipping refers to transitions mediated by intermediate state changes or environmental pathways rather than by a single direct trigger; indirect cascade tipping extends this logic to multi-step pathways; and cascade hopping highlights cases in which the perturbation effectively bypasses an apparently intermediate subsystem and activates a more distant one [80 ,81 ]. Closely related work on accelerating cascades emphasizes that one tipping event can shorten the timescale to subsequent ones, even when the network pathway is not strictly local [82 ]. These concepts are especially relevant for Earth-system applications, where teleconnections, unequal response times, and mediated feedbacks can make cascade pathways nonlocal and non-intuitive.

Compared with single-element tipping processes, compound tipping involving cascade effects poses far greater challenges for scientific analysis. These challenges span the identification, diagnosis, early warning, and prediction of tipping events, as well as the mathematical modeling and quantitative characterization of cascade dynamics themselves. For interacting subsystems, univariate indicators may therefore fail to capture changes arising from coupling structures and collective dynamics. Multivariate and interaction-aware EWS methods provide an important extension in this context. For example, detrended cross-correlation analysis (DCCA) quantifies scale-dependent correlations between nonstationary time series [61 ], enabling the detection of increasing co-fluctuations among subsystems prior to transitions. Similarly, multivariate covariance-based indicators and network-based approaches can characterize changes in collective resilience, synchronization, and teleconnection structures across interacting components [31 ,84 ]. These methods are particularly relevant for identifying cascading behavior in interconnected systems. However, their performance remains sensitive to factors such as detrending procedures, window-length selection, common external drivers, and pronounced nonstationarity.

Although the current framework is primarily designed for interactions between two coupled systems, it can be readily generalized to multiple systems [80 ,82 ]. For instance, the following system X2 can itself be treated as the leading system for a subsequent subsystem X3 . By iterating this structure, one can model cascades across multiple tipping elements, yielding clearly defined and directed cascade pathways. However, this framework overlooks two important aspects: (i) first, the direct influence of external drivers (such as global temperature) on the following systems, and (ii) second, the representation of feedback loops among various tipping elements due to the tipping cascade. Both aspects are critical for realistically modeling interactions among tipping elements in the Earth system.

Its strength lies in exploring how plausible feedback loops, interaction topologies, timescale heterogeneity, and overshoot forcing reshape the systemic risk. Its weakness is that critical thresholds Tcri ,i , interaction weights si j , and effective coupling strengths remain only partly constrained by observations and process-based models. We therefore use this framework primarily as a conceptual and sensitivity-analysis device, while reserving stronger empirical claims for links that are independently supported by physical understanding, observations, or dedicated simulations.

When coupling is introduced, the timing of tipping events can either advance or be delayed, depending on the net effect of positive and negative links. Through large ensembles of Monte Carlo simulations propagating parameter uncertainties, the authors investigated cascading tipping among four key elements, the GIS and WAIS, the AMOC, and the Amazon rainforest. Their findings reveal that interactions tend to destabilize the network, with the polar ice sheets often acting as initiators of cascading transitions. This aligns with evidence suggesting that several cryospheric tipping elements are already approaching critical thresholds [9 ,88 ]. Nevertheless, these results should still be read as a scenario-based risk amplification under a conceptual model, not as direct observational proof that the corresponding cascade sequence has already been realized in the Earth system.

The network model in Equation ( 11 ) was further extended to assess tipping risks under global temperature overshoot scenarios [89 ], in which temporary exceedance of the 1.5°C or 2°C targets, the so-called "overshoot," substantially elevates the probability of irreversible cascading events. Using the stylized network model, the authors demonstrated that triggering one tipping element (e.g., GIS collapse) during an overshoot phase can "lock in" that change, subsequently destabilizing other elements (e.g., the AMOC) by reducing their tipping thresholds. This process greatly increases the likelihood of a domino-like chain of climate system failures. Crucially, their analysis shows that temperature overshoots make damaging cascades roughly three times more likely than pathways that stabilize temperatures without an overshoot. They further estimate that temporary overshoots can increase tipping risks by up to 72% compared with non-overshoot scenarios, even if the long-term equilibrium temperature remains within the Paris Agreement range. These results underscore that avoiding even temporary exceedance of critical warming limits is essential to prevent self-reinforcing and potentially irreversible climate tipping cascades.

CHALLENGES

In this review, we have reviewed the fundamental concepts and recent advances in the study of tipping points and cascading effects from the perspective of dynamical systems and complexity science, with particular emphasis on EWS and their detection. A stylized network-based dynamic model was introduced to describe interactions and cascading behaviors among multiple climate tipping elements. Nevertheless, research in this field still faces substantial challenges, which can be broadly categorized into two aspects: data and methodology .

Data quantity

Thus far, most studies remain largely theoretical rather than data-driven, primarily due to the scarcity and heterogeneity of high-quality observational datasets suitable for diagnosing tipping points. A major challenge lies in the stringent data requirements of EWS methods, which typically demand long, high-resolution, and continuous time series. In practice, however, many climate records are either too short or contain missing values, resulting in discontinuities that severely hinder reliable EWS estimation. This limitation is particularly pronounced in the context of ocean observations, which are inherently sparse, spatially biased, and subject to substantial measurement uncertainties [90 ,91 ]. A key limitation arises from the uneven spatial distribution of oceanic observations across the globe, with vast data gaps in remote and deep-sea regions. For example, the Argo program [92 ], consisting of over 3000 autonomous profiling floats that continuously measure temperature and salinity, represents a cornerstone of the modern ocean observing system and complements satellite-based missions. Nevertheless, significant challenges persist: observational coverage remains biased toward the Northern Hemisphere due to logistical and funding constraints, while float operations are limited in the Southern Ocean and polar regions because of technical factors such as the lack of effective ice-avoidance mechanisms. These factors collectively lead to pronounced spatial heterogeneity and substantial observational gaps [93 ]. Moreover, direct observations of the deep ocean are intrinsically challenging. Extreme pressure, perpetual darkness, and inaccessibility make it exceptionally difficult to obtain highprecision, high-resolution in-situ measurements in abyssal environments. Consequently, available datasets are often biased or error-prone and are nonetheless used to initialize and validate largescale climate models. This dependence inevitably compromises the reliability of model simulations and amplifies uncertainties in future climate projections, particularly in processes such as deep and bottom water formation, which remain poorly constrained in most models [94 ].

In addition, all measurement systems are subject to instrumental errors, sensor drift, and calibration biases that can introduce systematic inaccuracies. Uncertainties also arise during the data processing and integration stages. For instance, gridding sparse point measurements or deriving geophysical quantities from satellite signals inevitably involves interpolation assumptions and empirical models that propagate error. Integrating heterogeneous datasets, from satellites, in situ measurements, and reanalysis products, further requires rigorous cross-calibration and homogenization to ensure consistency across spatial and temporal scales [93 ,95 ]. Moreover, uncertainties in marine ecosystem projections arise from multiple sources, including structural (model) uncertainty, initialization and internal variability, parameter uncertainty, and scenario dependence [91 ,96 ].

Methods of EWS and cascade

Despite their theoretical appeal, EWS methodologies face substantial practical and conceptual limitations that constrain their predictive reliability [31 ,56 ,97 ,98 ]. Dakos et al. [56 ] noted that not all regime shifts correspond to true tipping points, meaning that classical CSD-based indicators may fail to manifest even when abrupt transitions occur. A recent meta-analysis [31 ] reviewing over 200 studies found mixed results, with many reporting poor or negative predictive performance, especially in ecological contexts. Notably, nearly all negative cases relied solely on CSD-based early warnings. In a large-scale empirical test, Burthe et al. [97 ] analyzed long-term abundance time series from 55 taxa (126 datasets) across multiple trophic levels in marine and freshwater ecosystems. Their findings revealed that true positives were rare, only 9% (16 of 170) for variance and 13% (19 of 152) for autocorrelation, while false positives occurred more frequently than false negatives (53% vs. 38% for variance; 47% vs. 40% for autocorrelation). False detections were observed across all decades and ecosystem types. Additionally, most EWS techniques are developed for single external drivers, and their fundamental assumptions can fail when multiple drivers co-vary or interact [98 ].

To overcome the limitations of univariate CSD indicators, particularly for detecting interactions and cascading transitions, advanced multivariate methodologies have been proposed. Techniques such as DCCA [61 ] extend DFA to quantify cross-correlations between non-stationary signals. Despite their theoretical advantages, these methods present the following profound practical limitations: (i) First, DCCA remains highly susceptible to spurious crossovers induced by underlying trends (e.g., linear or periodic), which can fundamentally distort scaling results [99 ]. (ii) Second, DCCA detects correlation strength but provides limited insight into directional causality, making it difficult to distinguish genuine coupling between tipping elements from spurious cross-correlations induced by shared external forcings [100 ]. Alternative causality-based approaches, such as Granger causality and convergence cross mapping (CCM), partially address this by focusing on directional inference. However, they suffer from their own shortcomings: Granger causality typically assumes linear relationships, while CCM, though capable of capturing nonlinearity, requires massive data volumes and is highly sensitive to noise [101 ,102 ]. (iii) More generally, these multivariate and network-based indicators face substantial data requirements, demanding long, high-resolution time series that are rarely available in empirical ecological or Earth system observations [103 ]. Consequently, their performance in real-world cascading transitions remains poorly understood compared to idealized simulated models [104 ], underscoring the pressing need for robust, data-efficient indicators capable of capturing nonlinear, multi-scalar interactions.

A further challenge in studying cascading tipping phenomena lies in the parameterization of stylized network models [12 ]. In most existing frameworks, the estimation of critical thresholds and coupling strengths relies heavily on expert judgment rather than data-driven inference, which inevitably introduces subjectivity and constrains the generalizability of these models. For example, expert assessments have suggested that tropical coral reef systems may approach a tipping point when global mean temperature exceeds ∼1.5°C above pre-industrial levels [8 ]. However, widespread coral bleaching events have already been observed at warming levels of around 1.2°C [14 ], highlighting substantial uncertainty in expert-based estimates of critical thresholds and suggesting that some systems may respond more sensitively than previously assumed. At present, methods for empirically constraining such parameters from observed or simulated cascade events remain largely underdeveloped. Further complexity arises from the multi-timescale interactions among different climate tipping elements, which challenge both the formulation and calibration of network-based dynamical models. This methodological gap represents a fundamental limitation: without objective, data-informed calibration, stylized network models possess limited predictive capability and reduced practical utility for assessing systemic risks or designing effective resilience and mitigation strategies.

OPPORTUNITIES

Given these challenges, new methodological paradigms are urgently needed. In particular, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and complex network science [105 ] offers a promising avenue for advancing the study of tipping points and cascading transitions.

Artificial intelligence

Recent advances in AI have rapidly accelerated its application in ESS, leading to the emergence of an interdisciplinary AI-Earth Science research community [106 -108 ]. Discriminative models, such as Graph Convolutional Networks and Transformers, can automatically extract complex spatiotemporal dependencies from large-scale observational and reanalysis datasets, significantly improving the accuracy and efficiency of deterministic predictions [109 ,110 ].

Crucially, to address the challenge of current data scarcity, AI frameworks are evolving to harmonize disparate data scales. Generative AI approaches, including diffusion models and generative adversarial networks, have begun to transcend fundamental limitations of traditional data assimilation [111 -114 ]. These models can learn the posterior distribution of meteorological states, facilitating multi-fidelity learning that fuses abundant low-fidelity data (e.g., satellite proxies) with sparse high-fidelity measurements. Such advances collectively enhance the capacity to dynamically assimilate heterogeneous observations, improving data completeness, resolution, and spatiotemporal consistency, which are key prerequisites for a robust tipping point analysis.

Beyond data processing, AI holds significant promise for advancing the detection and prediction of tipping points in complex systems. Its advantages are reflected in three main aspects. (i) First, the integration of deep learning with generative techniques significantly enhances the robustness of EWS detection frameworks. By synthesizing realistic surrogate data to augment limited historical observations [115 ], these approaches enable models to learn from a vast array of simulated critical regimes. This data-rich training environment allows deep learning algorithms to substantially improve both the sensitivity and specificity of tipping point detection, while maintaining strong generalizability across diverse and previously unseen systems [69 ]. (ii) Second, AI methods are particularly well suited for capturing R-tipping events that are often missed by conventional EWS approaches, thereby enabling the detection of nonlinear abrupt transitions [70 ]. (iii) Third, advanced architectures allow for predictive modeling that extends beyond traditional EWS, enabling not only the estimation of tipping likelihoods but also the inference of critical thresholds [116 ,117 ].

While AI has emerged as a powerful tool for analyzing complex systems, many current datadriven models often function as "black boxes", offering limited insight into their internal decisionmaking processes [118 ,119 ]. Moreover, purely data-driven approaches may lack physical consistency, a critical limitation when applied to sparsely observed Earth system processes, such as marine tipping behavior, where training data rarely covers extreme regimes. Despite their impressive predictive capabilities, many AI-based Earth system models remain difficult to interpret mechanistically. Existing explainable AI (XAI) techniques, such as saliency maps or attention analyses, are often fundamentally correlation-based and may fail to identify the true causal drivers underlying tipping dynamics [120 ,121 ]. This issue is particularly critical for Earth system applications, where mechanistic understanding is essential for scientific credibility and policy-relevant decisionmaking. Furthermore, because observational records predominantly sample quasi-stable climate states, AI models may exhibit a limited extrapolation capability, when confronted with previously unseen critical transitions or cascading tipping events. Generative approaches may also inherit structural biases from climate simulations or reanalysis datasets, potentially leading to overconfident predictions in poorly observed regimes. Reliable uncertainty quantification therefore remains a major challenge for AI-driven tipping point prediction systems [122 ]. Future progress will likely require a tighter integration between explainable AI, causal inference frameworks, and physically grounded learning architectures to distinguish genuine EWS from spurious statistical correlations [123 ,124 ]. A promising pathway to overcome these limitations is the integration of physical laws directly into the learning architecture, an approach broadly termed scientific machine learning [125 ].

Two complementary strategies have emerged within this paradigm. (i) First, physics-informed neural networks (PINN) embed governing equations or conservation principles directly into the loss function as soft constraints, ensuring that predictions adhere to known physics even in datasparse regions [126 ]. Nevertheless, embedding physical constraints does not fully guarantee physically faithful behavior, particularly when the governing equations are themselves uncertain or only partially known. Many PINN-based approaches also face optimization and scalability challenges in high-dimensional geophysical systems. (ii) Second, structural priors can be encoded as inductive biases within the network architecture itself. For instance, GraphCast utilizes a meshbased graph neural network to represent longrange spatial interactions on a sphere, significantly extending the skill of medium-range weather forecasts [110 ]. Beyond improving predictive accuracy, progress in XAI offers a critical avenue to demystify these models, identifying the underlying drivers of tipping dynamics [127 -129 ]. In an operational climate risk assessment, improving interpretability and trustworthiness is particularly important because black-box predictions alone may be insufficient to support robust decisionmaking and climate adaptation strategies. Looking forward, the development of such interpretable, physics-aware AI systems represents an essential step toward building generalizable and trustworthy intelligence capable of simulating the complex, interconnected behavior of the Earth system, thereby offering critical insights into the study of its tipping points and cascading effects.

Complex networks method

In parallel, complex network theory has emerged as a powerful tool for analyzing the structure and dynamics of the Earth system [84 ,130 -135 ]. Pioneering works have established the utility of climate networks for forecasting major climate phenomena, most notably the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO). For instance, by analyzing the cooperative behavior and link density of the climate network in the Pacific basin, Gozolchiani et al. [136 ] and Ludescher et al. [137 ] demonstrated that network-based indicators could predict the onset of El Niño events over a year in advance, which significantly outperforms conventional dynamical models. These foundational studies highlight how topological changes in the functional network, such as the localization of teleconnections, serve as robust precursors for large-scale system transitions [138 ].

Beyond forecasting specific events, network approaches are increasingly pivotal for identifying critical thresholds and modeling cascading transitions among tipping elements. Recent research has moved from static topology to dynamic resilience. For example, Jiang et al. [139 ] reduced high-dimensional network data to lowdimensional dynamical forms to accurately predict tipping points. Network-based metrics, including normalized degree and path length, have also proven effective in detecting global tipping behaviors [72 ] and capturing teleconnections, such as the atmospheric bridge linking the Amazon rainforest, the Tibetan Plateau, and the WAIS [11 ]. Crucially, the risk of cascading transitions, where a tipping event in one subsystem triggers a domino effect, can be validly described by models of interdependent networks and interacting tipping elements [12 ,140 ]. Looking forward, integrating these network-based mechanistic insights with AI methodologies offers a powerful, data-rich framework for not only detecting isolated tipping points but also mapping the complex pathways of systemic collapse in the Earth system.
