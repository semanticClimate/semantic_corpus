Abstract

Global heatwave intensification under climate change will impact the nitrogen cycle; yet, its effect on active nitrifier groups or their interactions with viruses remains unclear. Using 13 CO2-DNA-based stable-isotope probing coupled with metagenomics, we show that elevated temperatures under heatwave conditions fundamentally restructure active nitrifying communities and their associated viruses in Yangtze River estuary upper tidal flats and adjacent agricultural soils. In tidal flats, sustained high temperature constrained nitrification by reducing the abundance of active ammonia-oxidizing archaea and bacteria (AOA, AOB) and canonical nitrite-oxidizing bacteria (NOB). This was accompanied by a shift in the active community from marine to more thermotolerant but less salt-tolerant terrestrial ecotypes. Conversely, heatwave conditions in agricultural soils suppressed AOB but enhanced nitrification activity in thermotolerant terrestrial AOA ecotypes. Across both ecosystems, inferred virus-nitrifier interactions were temperature dependent. 13 C-labeled nitrifier-infecting viruses exhibited coordinated shifts in virus-to-host abundance ratios and predicted lifestyles with their hosts, with sustained high temperatures reducing virus-to-host abundance ratios and favoring temperate infections, relative to higher abundance ratios and a greater proportion of predicted lytic cycles at lower temperatures. We identified AOA-infecting viruses that carry plastocyanin (pcy), encoding a key copperdependent electron carrier in the AOA respiratory chain, with conserved active sites and a predicted protein fold that supports its capacity for electron transfer, potentially augmenting host energy metabolism. Together, our findings demonstrate that prolonged heatwaves drive coupled shifts in nitrifier community composition and virus-host interaction strategies in a land-use-dependent manner, with implications for nitrogen transformations and ecosystem feedbacks under climate extremes.

Introduction

A heatwave is characterized as an extended period of unusually high temperatures, lasting from several days to months, with substantial effects on natural ecosystems, agricultural productivity, and human health [1-5]. As global climate change accelerates, the frequency, intensity, and duration of extreme heatwave events are increasing globally (IPCC, 2021). In many regions, heatwaves regularly reach temperatures exceeding 35 ◦ C-40 ◦ C with extremes reaching up to 45 ◦ C50 ◦ C, and durations that, in severe cases, may extend over 2 months [6-13]. Temperature is a critical driver of microbial metabolism, activity, and community structure, suggesting that heatwaves can drastically alter microbial community dynamics and interactions in terrestrial ecosystems [14-19]. These changes can, in turn, profoundly influence biogeochemical cycles and ecosystem services.

Coastal wetlands, serving as dynamic transition zones between terrestrial and marine ecosystems, face dual pressures from both rapid climate change and extensive anthropogenic disturbances. They represent critical climate-sensitive regions on Earth, simultaneously receiving substantial inputs of reactive nitrogen (N) from agricultural systems [20, 21]. The intensive conversion of coastal wetlands into agricultural systems across many regions of the world further disrupts soil structure and nutrient balance, thereby reducing the resilience of these wetland ecosystems to extreme climate conditions. However, the effects of heatwaves on the cycling of N-the nutrient having the most significant impact on local ecosystem functioning and global biogeochemical processes-remain poorly understood.

Ammonia- and nitrite-oxidizing microorganisms (i.e. nitrifiers) are responsible for the oxidation of >2000 Tg of N per year, constituting one of the largest N fluxes in the global N cycle budget [22]. Ammonia-oxidizing archaea and bacteria (AOA and AOB) convert ammonia (hereafter referred to as total ammonia, NH3 +NH4 + ) into nitrite (NO2 - ). Nitrite is subsequently oxidized to nitrate (NO3 - ) by nitrite-oxidizing bacteria (NOB), completing the two-step nitrification process. The recent identification of complete ammonia oxidizers (comammox, CMX), within the genus Nitrospira, demonstrates that in many aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems some microbes are capable of oxidizing ammonia to nitrate in a single cell [23, 24], but marine CMX strains have not yet been discovered. Although all nitrifiers generally depend on the energy generated from oxidation of ammonia and/or nitrite [25], some lineages exhibit metabolic versatility, such as the ability of utilizing urea, cyanate, or guanidine as ammonia sources [26-28]. Some NOB can even grow on hydrogen instead of nitrite [29, 30]. The distinct substrate preferences among different nitrifier lineages minimize direct substrate competition, enabling their coexistence in diverse environments [26-28]. Aside from the variety in compounds they can oxidize, major nitrifier lineages exhibit distinct thermal traits. For example, AOA and AOB generally show differences in temperature dependent nitrification activities in soils [3136]. However, the ecological implications of these thermal differences, particularly how different clades of nitrifiers respond to extreme heatwave events, remain largely unknown, despite their clear relevance to influencing community structure and activity in the context of climate change [37, 38].

In addition to bottom-up controls of nitrification, one of the major top-down controls on bacterial and archaeal communities is viral infection. Viruses, the most abundant biological entities on Earth, play a pivotal role in regulating the community structure, metabolic activity, and evolutionary dynamics of bacterial and archaeal populations, thereby significantly influencing the biogeochemical cycles driven by prokaryotes [16, 39-43]. For instance, in marine ecosystems, an estimated 10 28 viral infections occur daily, accounting for up to 50% of bacterial mortality [44], profoundly impacting prokaryotic ecology and biogeochemistry in the oceans. Viral infection also facilitates the horizontal transfer of key metabolic and regulatory genes in nitrifiers. Auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) encoding the archaeal variant of the AmoC subunit of ammonia monooxygenase, were first discovered in the global ocean virome [45], with virusencoded variants comprising up to half of the total amoC gene copies in cellular fraction metagenomes [46]. Given that previous physiological characterization has shown that AmoC likely maintains the integrity of a functional AMO complex under conditions of stress [47, 48], viral AmoC may enhance the adaptive capacity of AOA hosts in oligotrophic marine environments. In contrast, viruses infecting soil AOA appear to lack amoC AMGs and contain other AOA-specific AMGs [49, 50]. Furthermore, previous studies of pure cultures and environmental populations of nitrifier-infecting viruses, including those in lytic and lysogenic phases [45, 46, 49-55], suggests that viruses affect the physiology and genomic dynamics of nitrifying microorganisms. However, our understanding on how viral infections regulate nitrifier ecology and nitrification activity remains limited. Even less is known about how viral infections interact with bottom-up controls, such as elevated temperatures, to influence the composition, activity, and ecological function of nitrifying microorganisms.

Microcosm incubations are widely used to examine the effects of heatwave conditions on microbial communities [56-58]. In this study, we employed 13 C-DNA-based stable-isotope probing (SIP) with microcosms at 28 ◦ C and 37 ◦ C, respectively, to investigate how heatwave conditions reshape active nitrifier communities, alter nitrification activity, and modulate virus-host interactions in two distinct coastal landscapes. We focused on natural upper tidal flats and adjacent agricultural soils that were converted from tidal flats in the 1960s at the estuary of the Yangtze River-the longest river in Asia-to explore how land-use change mediates nitrification and host-virus dynamics under heatwave conditions. Significant changes in nitrification activity were observed in both ecosystems as temperature increased from moderate to high levels in summer. This was accompanied by clear shifts in active nitrifying populations and notable variations in their interactions with viruses, including different virus-to-host abundance ratios and predicted lytic versus lysogenic strategies. Notably, we identified viruses infecting AOA that contained the pcy gene, which encodes plastocyanin, a key electron carrier in the respiratory chain. Together, our results demonstrate that prolonged heatwaves restructure active nitrifier communities in a land-use-dependent manner, constraining nitrification in saline tidal flats mainly by reduced active nitrifier abundance, alongside an ecotype shift from marine to less salt-tolerant terrestrial counterparts, while enhancing nitrification in adjacent agricultural soils through the proliferation of thermotolerant archaeal ecotypes.

Material and methods

Site description and sample collection

The coastal upper tidal flat sampling site was located in Qidong City (30 ◦ 14 ′ N, 120 ◦ 09 ′ E), Jiangsu Province, China, within a typical subtropical monsoon climate zone. The region has a mean annual temperature of ∼15 ◦ C and annual precipitation of 1037.1 mm. During midsummer (July-August), air temperatures average∼28 ◦ C, with 95% of values between 26 ◦ C and 31 ◦ C (Nantong City, China). Summer heatwaves in the Yangtze River Delta can sustain temperatures >35 ◦ C, with local extremes up to 45 ◦ C, persisting for weeks to over 2 months [7, 9, 12, 13]. The study site is flooded only briefly during monthly spring tides (1-3 h), with soil temperatures at 0-15 cm tracking those of ambient air, differing by -0.7±2.3 ◦ C [59]. Since the 1960s, ∼266700 ha of tidal flats in Jiangsu Province have been reclaimed for agriculture. A paired sampling strategy was employed to collect three tidal flat and agricultural soil samples at a depth of 0-15 cm along the northern branch of the Yangtze River estuary fromwest to east in summer 2018 (Supplementary Fig. S1). At each site, five soil cores were randomly collected within a 10×10 m plot and combined into a composite sample. All samples were transported to the laboratory on ice packs for subsequent analyses of soil physicochemical properties, potential nitrification rates, and DNA-SIP microcosm incubations. Remaining subsamples were stored at -20 ◦ C.

Soil characterization, potential nitrification rate measurements, and DNA-stable isotope probing

Soil physicochemical properties were determined as described previously [60-63], including soil pH, electrical conductivity (EC), inorganic nitrogen species (NH4 + -N, and NO2 - -N plus NO3 - -N), soil organic matter (SOM), dissolved organic carbon (DOC), total dissolved nitrogen (TDN), total carbon (TC), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), and total potassium (TK). Potential nitrification rates were assayed at 5 ◦ C intervals from 5 ◦ C-40 ◦ C following established protocols [64]. DNA-SIP microcosms were set up to identify active nitrifiers [60, 61]. Although AOA and AOB are widely regarded as predominantly chemolithoautotrophs based on current physiological evidence, we cannot entirely exclude the potential for limited mixotrophic metabolism in some ammonia oxidizer populations [65]. Nevertheless, under the experimental conditions applied here, incorporation of 13 CO2 remains a robust indicator of autotrophic growth associated with active nitrification. Triplicate 120-ml serum bottles, each containing 12.0 g of sieved fresh soil (≈10.0 g dry weight), were amended with 50 μg NH4Cl-N g -1 dry soil to approximate in situ concentrations. Bottles were sealed and injected with 13 CO2 (99 atom% C; Sigma-Aldrich) or 12 CO2 (generated fromNa2CO3 acidification) for a final concentration of 5% (v/v) [60, 61, 66], and incubated at 28 ◦ C and 37 ◦ C in the dark for 8 weeks at 60% water-holding capacity [67, 68]. Headspaces were flushed with synthetic air (20% O2, 80% N2) and replenished with CO2 every 4 days. Ammonium, nitrite, and nitrate concentrations were measured weekly [61], and an additional NH4Cl-N was added to maintain 50 μg NH4Cl-N g -1 dry soil whenever concentrations fell below 6 μg g -1 .

After incubation, DNA was extracted from 0.5 g soil using the FastDNA Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals). Approximately 5 μg DNA

was fractionated by isopycnic ultracentrifugation in CsCl gradients (initial density 1.725 g ml -1 ; 177000×g, 44 h, 20 ◦ C; VTi65.2 rotor, Beckman Coulter). Fourteen fractions (∼360 μl each) were collected by water displacement using a syringe pump (New Era Pump Systems). Buoyant densities were determined with an AR200 refractometer (Reichert), and DNA was recovered by PEG 6000 precipitation before being dissolved in 30 μl TE buffer [69]. The abundance of amoA genes (encoding the alpha subunit of AMO) from AOA, AOB, and CMX in all fractions, as well as in pooled high buoyant density fractions (1.7331.745 g ml -1 ), was quantified by qPCR using a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad). Further details are provided in Supplementary methods.

Metagenomic sequencing and assembly

Metagenomic sequencing was performed on total DNA from original soil samples and pooled high-buoyant-density fractions (1.7331.745 g ml -1 ) [60, 61] from both 13 CO2-labeled and 12 CO2-control treatments, using the NovaSeq platform (Illumina) at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Assembly, binning, and annotation were conducted as described in Supplementary methods. In total, 28 AOA, 21 AOB, 3 CMX, and 15 canonical NOB MAGs of medium (≥50% completeness, ≤10% contamination) to high quality (≥90% completeness, ≤5% contamination) were recovered from in situ (total DNA extracted from original soil samples) and 13 C-DNA metagenomes. Phylogenomic and comparative genomic analyses are detailed in Supplementary methods.

Construction of in-house AmoA/NxrB database and community structure analysis

In addition to amoA and nxrB genes recovered from this study's metagenomes, homologs were compiled from public nitrifier genomes (1698 AOA, 528 AOB, 903 canonical NOB/CMX) in NCBI RefSeq, GTDB (April 2024), KEGG, and previous datasets [23, 70, 71]. Redundant sequences were dereplicated with CD-HIT [72] at 100% identity to generate a non-redundant amino acid dataset. The resulting sequences were aligned with MAFFT (version 7.407) [73], trimmed with Gblocks (version 0.91b) [74], and used to construct phylogenies in IQ-TREE (version 2.2.0) [75] with automatic model selection and 1000 ultrafast bootstraps, ensuring consistency with established nitrifier lineages [23, 70, 71, 76-79]. Community structure in 13 C-labeled SIP microcosms was assessed by mapping quality-filtered reads to this database with DIAMOND (version 2.0.2) [80] (≥80% identity, ≥100 bp coverage, e-value ≤1×10 -10 ). Reads were assigned to lineages based on phylogenetic placement and normalized by sequencing depth.

Identification, taxonomy, and lifestyle of the 13 C-labeled viral operational taxonomic units

Viral contigs ≥5 kb and≥10 kb were identified with VirSorter2 v2.2.3 [81] at the default cutoff of 0.5 for maximal sensitivity [82, 83]. Identified sequences were quality-checked with CheckV v0.9.1 [84], requiring at least one viral hallmark gene (e.g. capsid, portal, terminase, or integrase) or enrichment in unannotated genes [50], and potential host-derived regions were trimmed. Viral contigs sharing ≥95% nucleotide identity across ≥85% of the shorter contig were clustered into viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) using Cluster_genomes_5.1.pl [85], with the longest contig in each cluster selected as the representative. Taxonomic assignment was performed with PhaGCN2 v2.1 [86] following ICTV guidelines. The lifestyle (lytic vs. lysogenic) of nitrifier-infecting viruses at both temperatures was predicted with DeePhage v1.0 [82, 87, 88] at a cutoff value of 0.5.

To identify 13 C-labeled viral contigs, sequences recovered from pooled heavy DNA fractions of the 13 C incubations were considered 13 C-labeled only if their relative abundances were significantly higher than those in the corresponding heavy DNA fractions of the 12 C controls. Statistical significance was assessed using limma's empirical Bayes-moderated t test, yielding log2 fold-change values and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P values [89].

Virus-host linkage, gene-sharing network, and three-dimensional structure prediction of viral plastocyanin

"Ex situ" hosts of 13 C-labeled viral contigs were inferred from public nitrifier genomes (1698 AOA, 528 AOB, 903 NOB (including CMX); NCBI and GTDB, Sept 2024), and "in situ" hosts from the 67 nitrifier MAGs recovered in this study [90]. Host prediction combined three in silico approaches: (i) CRISPR matching: CRISPR spacers (≥25 bp) were extracted with CRT v1.1 [91], dereplicated at 100% identity (CD-HIT v4.8.1 [72]), and queried against viral contigs (100% identity, ≤1 mismatch, e-value ≤10 -5 ); (ii) homology-based inference: viral proteins were compared against the NCBI nr database with DIAMOND BLASTp (e-value <10 -5 ), and a viral host was assigned to nitrifiers when AOA, AOB, or NOB (including CMX) genomes accounted for the largest proportion of ORF "best-hit" [92]; and (iii) tRNA matching: host and viral tRNAs were predicted using tRNAscan-SE v1.3.1 [93], with viral tRNAs linked to nitrifiers only when uniquely present in nitrifiers and identical (or with ≤1 mismatch) to host tRNAs. Viral contigs supported by at least one method were classified as nitrifier-infecting viruses. A gene-sharing network of nitrifier-infecting viruses was constructed using vConTACT2 [94]. The 3D structure of viral plastocyanin was predicted with I-TASSER-MTD (https://seq2fun.dcmb.med.umich.edu/ I-TASSER/) and visualized and aligned with reference proteins using PyMOL v3.12 [95].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R v4.2.1. Independent-sample ttests were used to compare the relative abundances of nitrifierinfecting viruses and their hosts in 13 C-labeled metagenomes across temperature treatments. Effect sizes were calculated as Cohen's d [96] (mean difference/pooled SD). Effect sizes were classified as large (|d|>0.8), medium (0.5<|d|≤0.8), small (0.2<|d|≤0.5), or negligible (|d|≤0.2).

Results

Temperature-driven shifts in 13 C-labeled active nitrifiers in tidal flats and agricultural soils

Potential nitrification assays (PNA) revealed contrasting thermal optima: coastal upper tidal flat communities peaked at 25 ◦ C-30 ◦ C, whereas adjacent agricultural soils-converted from tidal flats in the 1960s-showed maximal activity at 35 ◦ C-40 ◦ C (Supplementary Fig. S1). These patterns are generally consistent with cultured nitrifier representatives, in which marine ecotypes typically display optima of ∼20 ◦ C-32 ◦ C [97-101] and terrestrial ecotypes of ∼35 ◦ C-42 ◦ C [35, 36, 102-105]. Soil microcosms for 13 C-DNA-SIP were established and incubated at 37 ◦ C for 8 weeks to mimic prolonged heatwave conditions and at 28 ◦ C for 8 weeks to represent average midsummer temperatures, enabling us to disentangle the respective effects of land-use history and elevated temperature warming on nitrifier community structure and virus-host interactions.

After 8 weeks of incubation, nitrification activity at both temperatures aligned with the optimal PNA temperatures identified for each habitat, with tidal flats and agricultural soils accumulating more nitrite/nitrate (NOx) at 28 ◦ C and 37 ◦ C, respectively (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. S1). Total DNA was extracted from the 12 C- and 13 C-incubatedmicrocosms, followed by ultracentrifugation and qPCR quantification of the distribution of amoA genes in CsCl gradients. These analyses revealed differential labeling of bacterial and archaeal ammonia oxidizers in 13 C-enriched high buoyant density fractions at 28 ◦ C and 37 ◦ C (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. S2). In DNA-SIP profiles of agricultural soil DNA, the relative abundance of AOB in high buoyant density fractions was lower at 37 ◦ C compared to 28 ◦ C (P < .01 for Agr-1), and the relative abundance of CMX was also reduced to less than 1.0×10 4 genes g -1 dry weight soil (P<.001) at elevated temperature (Fig. 1b). In contrast, the abundance of 13 C-labeled AOA increased by 4.3- to 8.5-fold at 37 ◦ C relative to 28 ◦ C, becoming the dominant and active ammonia oxidizers under sustained elevated temperature (Fig. 1b). These findings indicated that AOA contributed to a greater amount of nitrification activity under sustained high temperature conditions in agricultural soils. In contrast, in tidal flats soil, a sustained temperature at 37 ◦ C had an overall negative effect on all three major ammonia oxidizer lineages. CMX represented only a minor ammonia-oxidizing microbial groups in tidal flats, with CMX abundance falling below the detection limit (<1.0×10 2 copies g -1 dry weigh soil) at 37 ◦ C (Fig. 1b). For AOB, there was a notable decrease in 13 C-labeled abundance at 37 ◦ C compared to 28 ◦ C, with a reduction ranging from 43.8% to 47.1% (Fig. 1b). While also showing a decreasing trend with increased temperature, tidal flat AOA exhibited relatively higher tolerance to sustained temperature, with two of the three incubations (Tid-1 and Tid-3) showing no significant difference in 13 C-labeled abundance between 28 ◦ C and 37 ◦ C (P> .05) (Fig. 1b).

Community shift of 13 C-labeled nitrifiers at high temperature

Metagenomic analysis of the 13 C-DNA from DNA-SIP further substantiated distinct community shifts in responses to temperature differences between active nitrifiers in agricultural soils and tidal flats (Fig. 2). The relative abundance of major ammonia-oxidizing microbial lineages estimated from metagenomic analysis was consistent with qPCR quantification, supporting a temperature-driven transition in agricultural soils from AOB dominance at 28 ◦ C to AOA dominance at 37 ◦ C (Fig. 2a). Competitive read recruitment of 13 C-labeled metagenomes revealed that active nitrifying groups, including AOA, AOB, CMX, and canonical NOB, exclusively belonged to terrestrial ecotypes at both temperatures in agricultural settings (Fig. 2c). For instance, all 13 Clabeled AOA belonged to the order Nitrososphaerales which dominate in soil, and were predominantly composed of NS-Zeta and NS-Alpha sub-lineages in the labeling experiment, irrespective of temperature (Fig. 2c). Among AOB, Nitrosomonas communis/Nitrosomonas nitrosa was the most abundant sub-lineage at both temperatures, whereas the Nitrosospira sub-lineage was detected only at 28 ◦ C. In contrast, the relative abundance of Nitrosomonas ureae/Nitrosomonas oligotropha increased significantly (P< .001) at elevated temperature (Fig. 2c). For NOB, 13 C-labeled nxrB genes were primarily associated with Nitrobacter and Nitrospira lineage II at 28 ◦ C. However, at 37 ◦ C, the NOB community became more diverse, with a significant increase in the relative abundance of Nitrospira lineages I and V, as well as Nitrolancea (P<.001) (Fig. 2c). The 13 C-labeled CMX, detected only at 28 ◦ C, were exclusively affiliated with Clade A.2 (Fig. 2c), while clade B members showed no labeling at either temperature. Several nitrifier lineages, such as NS-Epsilon-2 AOA and CMX clade B, were abundant before microcosm incubation but not significantly labeled during DNA-SIP incubation (Fig. 2c).

Unlike the converted agricultural fields, tidal flats are transitional zones in estuarine ecosystems, harboring a mix of marine and terrestrial nitrifier populations. These habitats are characterized by high salinity, with electrical conductivity ranging from 493 to 1974 μs/cm (Supplementary Fig. S1). The sampling sites are only briefly inundated by seawater during the monthly spring tide (1-3 hours per month), so the surface temperature of the tidal flats for most of the month is nearly identical to the ambient air temperature, being on average 0.7±2.3 ◦ C lower than the air temperature [59]. We observed that sustained high temperature in tidal flats led to a significant shift in functional nitrifier communities, with active terrestrial ecotypes emerging at 37 ◦ C but absent at 28 ◦ C (Fig. 2b and c). Specifically, at 28 ◦ C, all active AOA in the tidal flats belonged to the typical marine groups NP-Gamma and NP-Theta (Fig. 2c). However, incubation at 37 ◦ C stimulated the activity and increased the relative abundance of the terrestrial group NS-Zeta and NS-Alpha in tidal flats, which accounted for 31-100% of the total 13 C-labeled AOA populations in the three samples (Fig. 2c). Likewise, when comparing incubations at 37 ◦ C to 28 ◦ C, the most active and dominant AOB ecotypes shifted from typical marine ecotypes N. marina/N. aestuarii to terrestrial ecotypes N. communis/N. nitrosa. (Fig. 2c). In addition, at both incubation temperatures, the 13 C-labeled active NOB predominantly belonged to Nitrospira lineage IV, which is widely detected in marine environments (Fig. 2c), whereas the 13 C-labeled Nitrolancea was detected only at 37 ◦ C (Fig. 2c). Consistently, Nitrolancea is commonly detected in engineered systems and some warm natural environments, and is represented by cultivated strains with optimal growth temperatures of ∼35 ◦ C-40 ◦ C [106, 107]. These temperature-driven lineage shifts of active nitrifiers were broadly consistent with genome-resolved analyses (see Supplementary results and discussion, Supplementary Figs S3-S10, and Supplementary Tables S1-S3).

Temperature-driven shifts in viral communities and virus-nitrifier interactions in agricultural soils and tidal flats

A total of 584 viral contigs ≥5 kbp in length, including 198 contigs ≥10 kbp, were recovered from the 13 C-labeled metagenomes of high buoyant density (1.733-1.745 g ml -1 ) following an 8-week DNA-SIP microcosm incubation. To avoid potential issues associated with short viral contigs, results obtained from the ≥5 kbp dataset [84, 108-111]

were cross-validated using the ≥10 kbp subset [39, 112]. Clustering at 95% sequence identity and 85% coverage yielded 484 vOTUs (Supplementary Table S4), of which only 28.8% (158 of 584) were annotated to families under the ICTV classification (Fig. 3a). The 13 Clabeled viral communities exhibited clear temperature-dependent shifts: in agricultural soils, Peduoviridae and Mesyanzhinovviridae dominated at 28 ◦ C, whereas Vilmaviridae was strongly enriched at 37 ◦ C; in tidal flats, Mesyanzhinovviridae dominated at 28 ◦ C, while Peduoviridae and Herelleviridae became more abundant at 37 ◦ C. Hostlinkage predictions using CRISPR spacer matches (3 linkages), tRNA gene similarity (15 linkages), and homologous gene searches (163 linkages) predicted 39, 63, and 60 contigs as viruses infecting AOA, AOB, and canonical NOB, respectively (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Table S4). Collectively, nitrifier-infecting viruses accounted for ∼50% of the abundance of all 13 C-labeled viral contigs across both ecosystems and temperatures, and these patterns were further validated with the ≥10 kbp dataset (Supplementary Fig. S11a and b). The remaining 13 C-labeled viral contigs with unresolved hosts may have originated from other microorganisms that incorporated the label through active 13 CO2 assimilation or cross-feeding. For example, four viral contigs linked to predatory Myxococcales and one to Bdellovibrionales were also detected, suggesting potential trophic interactions in which predators consumed 13 C-labeled nitrifiers, such as canonical NOB [113], or heterotrophs that incorporated the label through crossfeeding, thereby revealing a complex microbial food web fueled by autotrophic nitrification.

Across both ecosystems, inferred virus-nitrifier interactions based on predicted host affiliations and relative abundance patterns are strongly temperature dependent. Under high-temperature conditions, coordinated shifts in virus-to-host abundance ratios and predicted lifestyle strategies were observed, suggesting temperaturedriven restructuring of virus-host dynamics. Virus-to-nitrifier host abundance ratios were markedly higher at 28 ◦ C than at 37 ◦ C in both agricultural soils and tidal flats (Cohen's d>0.8; Fig. 3c and d), suggesting stronger relative viral representation at 28 ◦ C. Similar correlation patterns were also observed in the ≥10 kbp dataset (Supplementary Fig. S11c and d). Consistently, a higher proportion of predicted lytic nitrifier-infecting viruses was observed at 28 ◦ C compared to 37 ◦ C, whereas elevated temperature (37 ◦ C) increased the proportion of predicted lysogenic 13 C-labeled viruses across both ecosystems (Cohen's d>0.8; Fig. 3e). Although these differences did not reach statistical significance at the current sample size (n=3 per group, P>.05), the large effect sizes (Cohen's d>0.8) suggest that the observed shifts are biologically meaningful and warrant further validation with increased replication.

In addition to the functional viral contigs identified in this study, we performed a gene-sharing network analysis that incorporated a comprehensive reference dataset, including 324 previously reported nitrifier viruses, 2469 proviruses identified in 3121 nitrifier genomes from the NCBI and JGI databases, and 2411 viral contigs predicated to infect nitrifiers from the IMG-VR v4 database (Supplementary Fig. S12a). Our analysis revealed that 27.7% of the viral contigs recovered in this study were integrated into the resulting network (Supplementary Fig. S12a). As expected, viruses infecting the same group of nitrifiers tended to cluster together (Supplementary Fig. S12a). However, the network analysis also indicated that viruses associated with phylogenetically distinct nitrifying groups co-occurred within the same clusters, suggesting potential cross-infection (Supplementary Fig. S12a). Notably, one viral contig (3300022743|Ga0228699_1000093; 87.75 kbp) within such a cluster was predicted as infecting both NOB and AOB, which was supported by a spacer-to-protospacer match with NOB (with 1 mismatch), and by sharing the most abundant "best hit" homolog genes with AOB (Supplementary Fig. S12b).

Viral plastocyanin genes associated with copper-dependent energy metabolism of ammonia-oxidizing archaea

One AOA-infecting viral contig, designated as contig vContig_022 (7.13 kbp), was retrieved from the 13 C-labeled metagenome of agricultural soil microcosms incubated at 28 ◦ C. It contained a homolog of the AOA-specific plastocyanin (pcy) gene, which encodes one type of a single-domain cupredoxin [114], along with virusspecific genes and additional genes most closely related to AOA genomes (Fig. 4a). Another AOA virus contig (JGI virus accession no. RCM31_10001047; 35.02 kbp), recovered from planktonic microbial communities in the Amazon River plume, also contained a pcy gene flanked by viral genes and was most similar to those of AOA (Fig. 4a). In addition, two more viral contigs from saline lake (3300031223_assembled_Ga0307981_1000533; 37.15 kbp) and marine (3300009371 assembled Ga0118717_1001322; 47.19 kbp) environments also encoded AOA-type pcy genes, each similarly flanked by viral genes (Fig. 4a). These consistent patterns strongly support the possibility of virus-mediated horizontal transfer of AOA pcy genes across distinct environments. Plastocyanin functions as an electron carrier either as a standalone protein or potentially as an integral component of AOA respiratory chain complexes III and IV, and is sometimes fused in multicopper oxidase lineage 4 (MCO4) (Fig. 4b), although the physiological functions of MCO4

remain poorly understood [25]. Phylogenetic analysis showed that plastocyanin proteins encoded by these four viral contigs clustered within a well-supported clade alongside plastocyanin sequences from terrestrial and marine AOA, and clearly separated from other archaeal 1 and 2 domain cupredoxin-like proteins (Fig. 4c and d and Supplementary Fig. S13).

The X-ray crystal structure of a plastocyanin protein from N. maritimus SCM1 (Nmar_1307; PDB accession code 5FC9) revealed a single copper ion coordinated in a trigonal plane by one cysteine (Sγ Cys83-Cu = 2.25 Å) and two histidine residues (NδHis43-Cu = 2.01 Å and NδHis86-Cu = 2.07 Å) [115]. These three copper-coordinating sites are conserved across all plastocyanin proteins encoded by both AOAinfecting viral contigs and AOA genomes (Supplementary Fig. S14). Predicted structures of the two AOA-like plastocyanins from AOAinfecting viral contigs closely aligned with the characterized Xray crystal structure of Nmar_1307 (with Cov value 0.99 and TMscore 0.92), revealing conserved copper-binding ligands typical of plastocyanins (Fig. 4e and f). It is worth noting that virus-encoded AOA-like pcy genes were detected at 28 ◦ C but not in viral communities at 37 ◦ C. As such, our data do not support an association between virus-encoded pcy and host heat resistance. Instead, viral acquisition of plastocyanin genes more likely represents a strategy to modulate or augment host electron transport pathways, potentially benefiting viral replication and host metabolic performance under energy-limited or redox-variable conditions at 28 ◦ C.

Discussion

Global warming is driving an increase in the intensity, frequency, and duration of heatwaves worldwide, with profound effects on microbial communities and activities, thereby altering biogeochemical cycles and ecosystem functions. Nitrifying microorganisms control the transformation of fixed nitrogen species and fix carbon via chemosynthetic pathways, with an important contribution to global nitrogen and carbon cycling. Interactions between nitrifiers and viruses, the most abundant biological entities on Earth, are emerging as key factors regulating nitrifier ecology and functioning [45, 46, 4955, 116]. However, how heatwaves influence nitrifier communities and their viral interactions across diverse environments remains poorly understood, reflecting a largely unexplored intersection of bottom-up (temperature-driven) and top-down (viral infectionmediated) controls on microbial ecology. We conducted incubation experiments mimicking an extended heatwave condition of 8 weeks, matching the maximum duration observed locally [9, 13]. Our study revealed that heatwave temperature regimes substantially altered the composition of active members of nitrifying communities in tidal flats, with patterns distinct from those in adjacent agricultural soil ecosystems. Temperature-dependent differences were observed in virus-to-nitrifier host abundance ratios and in the relative proportions of predicted lytic and lysogenic cycles among 13 C-labeled viruses, suggesting the potential for heatwaves to reshape nitrifier-virus interactions and associated ecosystem functions. Notably, some AOA-infecting viruses carried pcy genes encoding key copper-dependent electron carriers involved in the AOA respiratory system, potentially modulating or supplementing electron transport under changing environmental conditions. These findings uncover a previously underappreciated layer of complexity in putative nitrifier-virus interactions under climate extremes, with important implications for understanding and predicting nitrogen and carbon cycling in land-sea transitional ecosystems in a warming world.

Coastal tidal flats are dynamic transition zones that harbor microbial communities originating from both adjacent marine and terrestrial ecosystems. These habitats host functionally redundant nitrifier ecotypes that coexist despite differing sensitives to major environmental variables such as temperature and salinity. This study revealed that sustained heatwave exposure triggered a pronounced shift of metabolically active nitrifying members in tidal flats, transitioning from typical marine ecotypes of AOA (NP-Gamma and NPTheta), AOB (N. marina/N. aestuarii), and canonical NOB (Nitrospira lineage IV) to their respective terrestrial ecotypes (NS-Zeta and NSAlpha; N. communis/N. nitrosa; and Nitrolancea). These terrestrial nitrifier ecotypes are known to exhibit greater thermal tolerance than their marine counterparts [35, 36, 97, 101, 103, 104, 117], consistent with their selective enrichment under heatwave conditions. However, despite their ability to occupy the niche following the replacement of marine nitrifiers, the overall abundance of active nitrifier was reduced at elevated temperature. This reduction, together with the limited salinity tolerance of these terrestrial ecotypes in the saline tidal flat environment [97, 101, 103, 118, 119], likely constrained their activity, resulting in lower nitrification as indicated by reduced NOX accumulation. In contrast, in adjacent agricultural soils, exposure to sustained heatwave-like temperatures from 28 ◦ C to 37 ◦ C resulted in a clear shift in 13 C-labeled active nitrifiers, with dominant soil AOB being replaced by terrestrial AOA lineages. These AOA ecotypes are known to encode thermotolerance-associated genes, and thus exhibit higher optimal growth temperatures than their terrestrial bacterial counterparts [31, 35, 36]. Unlike in tidal flats, the absence of salinity stress in agricultural soils may have allowed these thermotolerant AOA ecotypes to thrive under elevated temperatures, leading to increased nitrification activity. These results highlight that land-use context plays a critical role in modulating the impact of heatwaves on nitrifying communities and their ecosystem functions.

Beyond nitrifier community shifts, our results indicate that heatwave conditions restructured virus-nitrifier host interactions at multiple functional levels. At 28 ◦ C, elevated virus-to-host abundance ratios along with enrichment of predicted lytic nitrifier-infecting viruses likely reflected a virus-driven exploitation strategy aimed at maximizing replication through host metabolic takeover [120, 121]. In contrast, the elevated temperature (37 ◦ C) was associated with a decline in virusto-host abundance ratios and a higher relative frequency of predicted lysogenic phage. However, this pattern does not necessarily indicate a shift toward lysogeny. An alternative explanation is that heat stress may have triggered more frequent prophage induction, producing free viral particles that were then rapidly inactivated or degraded at warmer temperatures [16, 122]. This would artificially inflate the apparent proportion of lysogeny despite lower overall viral activity. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies showing that higher temperatures are associated with reduced viral proliferation and increased inactivation rates [123, 124]. Collectively, this coordinated shift between viruses and their nitrifier hosts underscores a temperature-dependent restructuring of virus-host dynamics, with significant implications for microbial community resilience and nutrient cycling in land-sea transitional ecosystems.

Copper plays a critical role in regulating AOA metabolism, with plastocyanin-encoding a type 1 blue copper-binding domain serving as a key electron carrier widely encoded across AOA lineages and essential for their copper-centric respiratory systems [25, 125, 126]. In this study, we identified AOA-specific plastocyanin genes within viral contigs. Phylogenetic analysis and domain alignments showed that these viral plastocyanin sequences clustered closely with those from AOA genomes and shared conserved copper-binding sites, supporting a potential functional role in electron transfer. AOA typically harbor multiple copies of plastocyanin genes across lineages. For instance, the marine AOA model species Nitrosopumilus maritimus strain SCM1 encodes 17 small blue copper-containing plastocyanins, representing multiple homologs that are differentially expressed under varying growth conditions [47]. This suggests functional diversification of plastocyanins, potentially enabling AOA to fine-tune their electron transport systems in response to environmental fluctuations. The presence of plastocyanin genes in AOA-infecting viruses may thus reflect a viral strategy to supplement or stabilize host electron transport, enhancing host adaptability and potentially promoting viral propagation. In addition to plastocyanins, Lee et al. recently reported that AOA-infecting viruses encode multicopper oxidase lineage 1 (MCO1) genes [49, 50] which may also be implicated in augmenting AOA energy metabolism. Analogous to cyanophages carrying AMGs encoding key photosystem components to boost host photosynthetic efficiency under stress [127], or phages that harbor nitrogen fixation genes to induce heterocyst formation in Anabaena and Nostoc under nitrogen-limited conditions [128], AOA viruses may broadly transduce genes encoding coppercontaining proteins involved in electron transfer and core energy metabolism in AOA for mutual benefit of both host and virus.

Ammonia oxidizers and nitrite oxidizers often coexist in close proximity or form aggregates in the environment to establish syntrophic associations [129-133], where AOB/AOA supply nitrite as an energy source for NOB, while NOB help alleviate nitrite toxicity for AOB/AOA or provide ammonia through urea or cyanate hydrolysis [27, 134]. In this study, our data suggests AOB and NOB could share similar viruses, or even a single virus could infect multiple nitrifier hosts. This is consistent with the findings of recent proximity-ligation-based metagenomic Hi-C studies that a single virus could infect microbial hosts that belong to phylogenetically distantly related microbial domains [135, 136], especially those in aggregates or biofilms, such as the ANME (anaerobic methanotrophic archaea)-SRB (sulfatereducing bacteria) or SOB (sulfur-oxidizing bacteria)-SRB pairs found in dense hydrothermal mats [136]. Surveys of public datasets have also predicted viruses capable of interacting with hosts across domains in diverse ecosystems characterized by syntrophic biofilms [136]. Such patterns suggest that physical proximity and shared metabolic networks within aggregates may promote viral encounters with phylogenetically diverse hosts, eventually selecting for viruses with broader host ranges through ecological and evolutionary mechanisms, such as repeated co-infection and spatial constraints [136]. While derived from a metagenome-based study, this work suggests that an individual virus could potentially infect both AOB and NOB, as well as different populations within a syntrophic microbial community.

Conclusions

Our study shows that prolonged heatwaves fundamentally reorganize active nitrifying communities and their interactions with viruses in tidal flats and agricultural soils, directly altering nitrification under extreme thermal stress. Beyond reshaping nitrifier composition and activity, temperature-dependent virus-nitrifier interactions emerged as an additional layer regulating nitrifier ecology and function. Under low temperature conditions, higher virus-to-host abundance ratios, more frequent predicted lytic infections, and the presence of viral pcy suggest that viral infections may augment host energy metabolism while simultaneously increasing host mortality, thereby modulating nitrifier population turnover and nitrification rates. In contrast, incubation under high temperature conditions favored temperate viral infection strategies, potentially reducing virus-induced mortality while diverting host resources toward viral genome replication without functional compensation for energetically constrained nitrification.

These temperature-driven shifts in both nitrifier composition and viral infection strategies are also likely to generate distinct ecosystem feedbacks. In agricultural soils, thermally stimulated AOA, with intrinsically lower N2O yields [137], may result in lower nitrification-derived N2O emissions even as accelerated ammonia oxidation enhances nitrate leaching and lowers crop nitrogen-use efficiency, while warming-induced transitions toward temperate viral lifestyles may weaken lytic dissolved organic matter release and alter soil carbon-nitrogen coupling. In tidal flats, the replacement of marine nitrifiers by more thermotolerant but less salt-adapted terrestrial ecotypes may suppress nitrification and increase the export of reduced nitrogen to estuarine waters, with accompanying viral shifts further influencing organic matter processing at the land-sea interface. Because heatwaves often coincide with drought or high humidity, producing dry or humid heatwaves [138], their combined impacts on nitrogen-cycling microbiomes remain to be fully elucidated. Moreover, beyond nitrification, heatwaves also affect microbial processes linked to other nutrient cycles, greenhouse gas emissions, and carbon sequestration [18], thereby contributing to biogeochemical feedback loops that may further amplify or mitigate climate change. Together, advancing our understanding of these mechanisms will improve the accuracy of models and projections regarding the interactions between extreme climate events and ecosystem services, which are ultimately underpinned by the resistance, resilience, and functionality of microbial communities.
