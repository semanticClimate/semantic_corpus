Imminent rapid decline of the Indonesian Throughflow after reaching a turning point of CO2 concentration

Received: 16 October 2024

Accepted: 13 November 2025

Check for updates

Shijian Hu 1,2,3,4 , Xi Lu 3 , Janet Sprintall 5 , Cong Guan 1,2,3,4 ,

Xiaozhou Ruan 6 & FanWang 3,4

The Indonesian Throughflow (ITF) represents the only tropical inter-ocean pathway of global ocean circulation and plays a central role in the Earth's climate system. Climate models suggest that the ITF would be weakened under global warming, but historical observations indicate a contradictory trend. Here using CMIP6 simulations, we find that the response of the ITF to increasing atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) concentration is nonlinear and exhibits an abrupt rapid decline after surpassing a turning point. The ITF's decline can be explained by the response of its halosteric and thermosteric components to rising CO2 levels associated with changes in winds and precipitation. The current level of CO2 concentration has reached the turning point, indicating that a rapid decline of ITF and a transition of the Indo-Pacific state is likely imminent despite the remaining uncertainties. These findings have implications for understanding climate tipping elements and global ocean circulation under global warming.

The Indo-Pacific region is the only pathway that connects different ocean basins in the tropics and plays a central role in the Earth's climate system 1-3 . The cross-basin oceanic flow from the Pacific to the Indian Ocean, termed the Indonesian Throughflow (ITF), controls the inter-ocean exchange of heat and fresh water between the Pacific Ocean and Indian Ocean, affects the heat uptake of the tropical oceans and the storage of extra heat due to greenhouse gas emissions, and hence plays a crucial role in the global climate system 4-8 . In addition, the ITF serves as the key to the Global Meridional Overturning Circulation (GMOC) system 1,9,10 . The strengthening and weakening of the ITF lead to a fluctuation of the inter-basin exchange between the Pacific and Indian Oceans, change the GMOC and shape the Indo-Pacific and global marine environment and climate 6-8,11 .

Previous studies suggest that the ITF possesses significant natural variability on multi-time scales, e.g., intra-seasonal variability triggered by the Madden-Julian Oscillation 12 , interannual variability associated with El Niño-Southern Oscillation 13-17 , decadal variability related to the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO) and the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) 11,18-23 , as well as long-term changes due to external forcing 4,24-26 . Climate modes affect the ITF through both the wind forcing and salinity changes related to freshwater forcing 11,16,27-29 . The salinity effect plays an essential role in the interannual variability and decadal trend of the ITF transport and properties 11,16 . Recent studies suggest that the salinity effect is important in determining the multi-decadal change of the ITF as well 30,31 .

In the context of global warming, climate models suggest that the ITF will be weakened significantly due to a number of factors including a weakening of the Walker Circulation in the Pacific Ocean 4 ; and/or a decrease in the deep waters entering the Pacific basin at ~ 40°S with subsequent reduction in the net deep ocean upwelling in the tropical and South Pacific 24,32 ; and/or remote forcing in the North Atlantic Ocean 26 . It is worth noting that the ITF is in general affected by either wind forcing-related dynamics, and/or buoyancy-forcing related processes that primarily affect the halosteric component 30 . In contrast to the climate models, historical observations suggest that the ITF transport showed no significant trend during the past century or even an increasing trend during the recent decades when the ocean has been rapidly warming 11,30,31,33,34 . Given that greenhouse gas concentrations are expected to continue rising over the next century as they have over the past hundred years, the inconsistency between historical ITF trends and future projections raises critical questions. It is therefore essential to understand the response of ITF to rising greenhouse gas concentration, and at what threshold of greenhouse gas concentration will the long-term trend of ITF become discernible.

Several tipping points may be triggered when global temperatures rise above 1.5 °C compared with the pre-industrial level 35-37 . Recent studies suggest that the increase of global temperature will exceed 1.5 °C in less than 10 years according to the current emissions trajectory 38 . The ocean circulation system shows significant changes in response to global warming 39 , and the collapse of the AMOC is thought to be one of the most prominent climate tipping elements 35-37,40,41 . A long-term strengthening or weakening of the ITF could lead to state transitions of the Indian Ocean and Pacific Ocean (IPO) system: a strong ITF means extensive exchange of heat, freshwater, nutrients and carbon between the two oceans, while a collapsed ITF represents a relatively isolated IPO system. However, it remains uncertain whether the ITF can be classified as one of the tipping elements or not.

In this study, we aim to investigate the response of the ITF to increasing atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2), examine the independent responses of halosteric and thermosteric components of the ITF, and explore the role of the ITF as a potential climate tipping element, using numerical simulations from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) and in situ observations.

Results

The ITF response to increasing CO2 concentration

We mainly use simulations from the historical runs (Hist, 1850-2014) and idealized 1% annually increasing CO2 concentrations experiments (1pctCO2, 1850-1999) from 25 CMIP6 coupled climate models to examine the ITF response to increasing CO2 concentration. First, we validated the ITF strength in the CMIP6 models and found that the multi-model mean reasonably reproduces the observed ITF 30 and captures its mean structure and seasonal cycle. The vertical structure of the climatological mean zonal currents across the 114 °E section and their changes in 1pctCO2 relative to the Hist runs are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1a. The mean ITF in the CMIP6 models shows a core between 11 °S and 13 °S with maximum velocity greater than 20 cm/s. The ITF transport averaged over the 150 years in the Hist runs of CMIP6 is about - 13Sv (hereafter negative for westward into the Indian Ocean), quite close to the - 15Sv determined from direct observations 42 . Supplementary Fig. S1b presents a comparison of the seasonal cycle of ITF transports from the historical runs of CMIP6 models and the geostrophic calculations using observations-based datesets 30 . The observed seasonal cycle of ITF transports is well reproduced in the CMIP6 Hist runs. The correlation coefficient is 0.82 (significant at a 99% confidence level) between the ensemble mean of CMIP6 models and the observed geostrophic transport.

To separate the halosteric and thermosteric components, we calculated the total transport of the ITF and geostrophic transport of the ITF with modeled temperature and salinity. The time series of ITF transport in the 1pctCO2 experiments and Hist runs of CMIP6 and their halosteric (ITFS) and thermosteric (ITFT) components are presented in Fig. 1.

Although the geostrophic transport is smaller than the total transport, the ITF transports from the geostrophic calculations (Fig. 1a, d) are in good agreement with the total transport in both the 1pctCO2

experiments and the Hist runs (Supplementary Fig. S2), indicating that the geostrophic current is a major component of the ITF at 114 °E.

The natural variation of the ITF transports, defined as the standard deviation of the detrended time series, is about 0.45Sv in both the 1pctCO2 experiments and the Hist runs. However, the long-term trends in the 1pctCO2 experiments are clearly different from that in the Hist runs. As evident, the ITF transport in the Hist runs shows no significant trend during the past 150 years (Fig. 1d). By comparison, the ITF transport in the 1pctCO2 experiments shows a persistent weakening trend of about 0.3 Sv per decade, i.e., 4 Sv over 150 years, which is statistically significant at a 99% confidence level (Fig. 1a).

The trend of the ITF transport in the Hist runs is very weak and much smaller than the uncertainty: the linear trend of the total ITF is - 0.01 ± 0.04 Sv decade -1 (positive for decreasing transport), the trend of ITFS is -0.02±0.13Sv decade -1 , while the linear trend of ITFT is 0.01 ± 0.13 Sv decade -1 (Fig. 1f-h). By contrast, the ITF transport in the 1pctCO2 experiments shows a significant decreasing trend: the linear trend of total ITF is 0.28±0.11 Sv decade -1 , with the trend of ITFS at - 0.32 ± 0.37 Sv decade -1 , while the linear trend of ITFT is 0.60 ± 0.38 Sv decade -1 . Hence, the halosteric and thermosteric components of the ITF transport show significant opposite trends in response to the increasing CO2 concentration.

Although the trends of ITF transport in Hist runs are much weaker than that in the 1pctCO2 experiments, the uncertainties in the two experiments have a similar order of O(0.1) Sv decade -1 , and natural variability in the two experiments have the same magnitude (0.45 Sv) as well. This implies that the natural variability in the simulated ITF transport in the two experiments are comparable and reasonable, and the significant difference between the long-term trends in ITF transport is due to their difference in the external forcing, i.e., the CO2 concentrations in the two experiments.

The ITF transport and its halosteric and thermosteric components as a function of CO2 forcing are shown in Fig. 2. It should be noted that in CMIP6 models, the CO2 forcing is represented by the total atmospheric mass of CO2 (measured in Kg), which also serves as a variable indicating the concentration of atmospheric CO2. The thermosteric component of ITF transport shows a decreasing ITF transport trend with increased CO2 forcing at a rate of 0.86 Sv per 10 15 Kg (Fig. 2c, b). By comparison, the halosteric ITF transport shows an increasing ITF transport trend with enhanced CO2 forcing at a rate of -0.37 Sv per 10 15 Kg (Figs. 2c, b). As a result, the total ITF transport is decreased with CO2 forcing at a rate of approximately 0.62 Sv per 10 15 Kg (Fig. 2a, b).

Interestingly, the decline of ITF transport in response to atmospheric CO2 forcing is weak when the CO2 mass is less than approximately 3× 10 15 Kg (i.e., 385 ppm, Supplementary Fig. S3a) or greater than 8 × 10 15 Kg (red dots in Supplementary Fig. S3b), but is very rapid in between, indicating a nonlinear response of the ITF transport and its components to external CO2 forcing. The decline trend of total ITF transport (ITFTS) in response to CO2 forcing is 0.81 Sv per 10 15 Kg when 3× 10 15 Kg <CO2 mass <8× 10 15 Kg, but drops to 0.31Sv per 10 15 Kg when CO2 mass is greater than 8 × 10 15 Kg, and 0.21 Sv per 10 15 Kg when CO2 mass is less than 3 × 10 15 Kg (Fig. 2b). The decline rate of the ITF shows a peak of 0.93Sv per 10 15 Kg when CO2 mass is about 4.5 × 10 15 Kg, which is about 4 times of that when CO2 before 3 × 10 15 Kg and 3 times of that when CO2 after 8 × 10 15 Kg. These features suggest that a CO2 mass of ~ 3× 10 15 Kg and 8× 10 15 Kg might provide two important turning points (Fig. 2). The comparison between the ITF response to the CO2 forcing in the 1pctCO2 experiments and the Hist runs may explain why the ITF transport in the Hist runs shows no significant trend. As we can see in Fig. 2, the CO2 forcing in the Hist runs is mostly less than 3× 10 15 Kg below the first turning point, and hence the response of the ITF is certainly much weaker than that in the 1pctCO2 experiments.

In addition, the ITF transport versus global mean surface temperature (GMST), and GMST as a function of CO2 mass, is shown in Supplementary Fig. S4. We find that the GMST increases linearly with the increasing of CO2 mass with no turning points (Supplementary Fig. S4a), but the GMST shows a similar qualitative response as a function of CO2 concentration, with an initial slow response followed by a faster growth and two turning points (Supplementary Fig. S4b). The model spread in the simulated ITF-GMST relationship is notable, but the overall trend of ITF in response to GMST is much greater than the model spread (Supplementary Fig. S4b). The consistency of ITFCO2 and ITF-GMST is not surprising, considering the linear relationship between GMST and CO2 mass. The ITF anomalies (minus their corresponding mean values) in three CO2 scenarios, including 1pctCO2, Hist, and the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) 585, are plotted together for comparison, and these scenarios indicate a similar ITF response to CO2 forcing (Supplementary Fig. S4c).

To further examine the emerging time of the long-term trend of the ITF from its natural variability, we have calculated the running trend of the ITF transport over a 30-year window in both the CMIP historic runs and 1pctCO2 experiments. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S5, the running trend in the Hist runs fluctuates within a range of roughly - 2 to 2 Sv/century, which is a result of natural variability. By contrast, in the 1pctCO2 experiments, the 30-year running trend reaches 4 Sv/century rapidly, and the fluctuations are masked by the significant decreasing trend when the CO2 reaches the first turning point, indicating that the first turning point is indeed the emerging time of a weakening ITF trend that is distinct from its natural variability.

Observations suggest that the CO2 mass has increased from 2.46 × 10 15 Kg in 1958 to 3.10 × 10 15 Kg in 2014, indicating an increasing rate of CO2 mass of about 1.12 × 10 15 Kg per century during the past half a century (Supplementary Fig. S3). Observations indicate that the annual mean CO2 in 2023 was approximately 3.30× 10 15 Kg, which implies an increase of about 0.84 × 10 15 Kg since 1958. With a mean rate of 0.42 Sv per 10 15 Kg, the increase of CO2 by the year 2023 would lead to a decrease of about 0.4 Sv in the ITF transport, which is comparable to the natural variability, i.e., 0.45Sv. Assuming a future rate of CO2 increase similar to that observed in 2014, the mass of CO2 would exceed 4.33× 10 15 Kg a century later. As shown in Fig.2a, the ITF will finally be stable at only about 6.9Sv when the CO2 is beyond 8.5× 10 15 Kg, indicating that the exchange between the Indian Ocean and Pacific Ocean would be nearly half of that at present, and the IPO system would be relatively isolated. This phase transition is hence essential for the Indo-Pacific environment and the global climate system.

Response of wind and buoyancy forcing to increasing CO2

A further question arises as to how the wind and buoyancy forcing, which exert direct control over variations in ITF transport, respond to external CO2 forcing. This is specifically relevant given that the ITF is determined by the Indo-Pacific pressure gradient related to the difference in steric height between the western Pacific Ocean and eastern Indian Ocean, which is further regulated by wind forcing and buoyancy forcing (precipitation) in the Indo-Pacific Ocean 30 .

Given that the ITF's response is determined by the response of both the thermosteric component and halosteric component, we first examine the response of oceanic temperature and salinity in the IndoPacific Ocean to increasing CO2 concentration. Both the WPO and EIO show a significant warming trend in the 1pctCO2 experiments, but the warming in the EIO is much more rapid than that in the WPO, leading to a decreased temperature difference between the EIO and WPO with the increased CO2 concentration (Supplementary Fig. S6c and S6e). By contrast, the difference in salinity between the EIO and WPO is increased as the CO2 concentration increases due to a significant freshening in WPO but with no significant change in salinity in the EIO (Supplementary Fig. S6d and S6f).

Previous studies suggest that the temperature in the tropical IndoPacific Ocean is mainly related to a convergence of warm water due to zonal wind stress, while salinity changes in the upper layer ocean are mainly associated with surface freshwater input due to precipitation 43,44 . Hence, we further examine the response of the zonal wind stress and precipitation in the tropical Indo-Pacific Ocean to the CO2 forcing in the 1pctCO2 experiments.

As shown in Fig. 3, zonal wind stress in both the EIO and WPO shows an increasing trend, i.e., weakening of westward trade winds, in the 1pctCO2 experiments, leading to a significant decreasing trend in the difference of zonal wind stress between the EIO and WPO. Note that, for the wind forcing, the ITF variability on decadal and longer time scales is likely controlled by mostly the Pacific winds and then the southeastern Indian Ocean 6 . The weakening of trade winds in the western Pacific Ocean leads to weakening convergence of warm water in the upstream region of the ITF in the Pacific and reduces the pressure gradient between the western Pacific Ocean and eastern Indian Ocean that drives the long-term ITF 45 . In comparison, the trends of precipitation in the two regions are clearly opposite: significantly increased precipitation in the WPO but decreased precipitation in the EIO. This opposite response causes an increasing trend of precipitation difference between the EIO and WPO (Fig. 3d). An intermediate point exists at 5 × 10 15 kg of CO2, where the wind stress and precipitation anomaly changes sign, but this is mainly because we display anomalies relative to a mean value over a CO2 range of 2 to 10 × 10 15 kg.

Like the ITF transport, the response of wind forcing and precipitation in the Indo-Pacific Ocean to CO2 forcing also shows turning points (Fig. 3b, d). During the stages with atmospheric CO2 mass less than 3× 10 15 Kg or massive atmospheric CO2 mass greater than 8× 10 15 Kg, the responses of both wind forcing and precipitation are weak. But between the two turning points, both the forcings, particularly the wind forcing, are very sensitive to the increasing of CO2 mass (Fig. 3b, d). After exceeding the second turning point, the responses of wind stress and precipitation differences in the western Pacific and eastern Indian Ocean to CO2 are both weakened, leading to a reduced ITF response, which may be associated with changes in the background state and the weakened Indo-Pacific exchange under this regime. Therefore, the responses of wind stress and precipitation to increasing CO2 concentration explain the opposite and nonlinear response of the ITF's halosteric and thermosteric components to increasing CO2 concentration.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the response of the ITF transport to increasing CO2 concentration using simulations from the Hist runs and 1pctCO2 experiments from CMIP6 models, and examined the mechanism of the ITF response from the perspective of the halosteric and thermosteric components of the ITF transport. The ensemble mean ITF transport in the CMIP6 models are validated through comparison with direct in situ observations.

We find that the response of the ITF transport to CO2 forcing is nonlinear and strongly related to the level of CO2 concentration, and two turning points exist. Figure 4a presents a schematic diagram illustrating the response of the ITF to CO2 forcing, concisely demonstrating the nonlinear response process of the ITF across different CO2 phases as shown in CMIP6 models. The trend of the ITF transport in response to CO2 forcing is weak when the total CO2 mass is less than the first turning point 3 × 10 15 Kg or greater than the second turning point 8× 10 15 Kg, but is very rapid in between. Significantly, we note that the level of current CO2 mass has reached 3 × 10 15 Kg, i.e., the first turning point of ITF's response to CO2, by the end of Hist run in 2014 (Supplementary Fig. S3), suggesting that we may have recently entered a "Transition state" when the ITF will begin to rapidly decrease. In a state when the ITF is as strong as present, the IPO system is in an "Exchange state": massive transport of heat and water mass between the two oceans would significantly shape the IPO system and exert influence on a global scale (Fig. 4a). By contrast, in a state when the ITF is very weak due to increasing CO2 concentration, the IPO system is in an "Isolate state", in which the IPO and global ocean circulation and climate system might be very different (Fig. 4a). The decline of the ITF presented in our study is in agreement with the projection runs and previous studies that show a weakening trend of the ITF in the context of global warming 4,17 , but the turning points and the rapid decline after the first turning point have not been reported previously.

It is well known that several tipping points exist under the context of global warming, and a potential AMOC collapse is one of the climate tipping elements 35-37,40,41,46 . The decline rate of the ITF is expected to not only reshape the marine environment within the Indo-Pacific Oceans, but also exert significant influence on the GMOC and global climate system. For example, the weakening trend or even shutdown of ITF might reduce the GMOC, lead to faster warming in the North Pacific Ocean, and decrease the heat uptake of the IPO system. So, the nonlinear response of the ITF to increasing CO2 concentration and the two turning points are of much significance for the earth's climate system, implying that the collapse of the ITF might be also a potential climate tipping element.

The decreasing trend of the ITF in the 1pctCO2 experiments is a result of the opposite response from the halosteric and thermosteric components to increasing CO2 concentration: there is a significant decreasing trend in the ITF thermosteric component but a smaller increasing trend in the halosteric component. The combined response of IndoPacific wind forcing and precipitation to CO2 forcing, as well as the thermosteric and halosteric components, determines the nonlinear decreasing trend of ITF and its two turning points (Fig. 4). Several essential issues arise in our study that need to be addressed in future research. For example, the response of the ITF, including its thermosteric component and salinity-related halosteric component, is nonlinear. However, questions persist regarding the physical mechanisms behind the ITF's decline and the presence of turning points. Previous studies suggested that changes in the deep ocean upwelling associated with the AMOC also play an important role in the long-term trend of the ITF in addition to the changes in the Indo-Pacific wind forcing 24,32 , implying that the response of the halosteric and thermosteric ITF to CO2 levels might occur through both wind forcing and deep ocean upwelling. Some studies suggest that the response of global mean surface air temperature to CO2 perturbations shows a fast timescale of a decade or two, followed by a slow timescale 47 . Even though the response of the ITF to CO2 is expected to be different from that of global mean temperature, the fast and slow response processes might be important in determining the turning points. The time scales of the ITF's response to the abrupt increasing of CO2 are examined using the abrupt 4 × CO2 runs combining the Hist runs. We found that the adjustment of the ITF lasted for about 40 years in the initial stage after abrupt 4 ×CO2 and then the ITF reaches a dynamic stable state (Supplementary Fig. S7), indicating that the projected rate of ITF weakening in these models is also influenced by multidecadal variability. It should be noted that, during the rapid weakening of the ITF after the abrupt CO2 increase in the 4×CO2 runs, the differences among the models are minimal relative to other phases, indicating that different models undergo roughly the same adjustment period (Supplementary Fig. S7). The results from the 4×CO2 experiment are consistent with and corroborate the findings of the second turning point observed in the 1pctCO2 experiment (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S7). Further in-depth research is still needed to understand the physical processes and mechanisms underlying these two turning points.

These results may have implications for understanding the longterm trend of the historical ITF transport under a global warming scenario when the CO2 concentration increases, as well as for future projections of not only the IPO system but also the global climate system. Given that the rapid changes in the ITF in response to changes in CO2 concentration would lead to changes in the inter-basin exchange of heat and freshwater between the Indian Ocean and Pacific Ocean, turning points might also occur for other Indo-Pacific climate indices, and further studies are needed.

However, as noted, large uncertainty still exists among the climate models, particularly the idealized 1pctCO2 experiment, in which aerosols and other factors such as land use changes and other greenhouse gases are not considered. Hence, the results represent an idealized scenario rather than a direct prediction of future conditions, and other anthropogenic sources of radiative forcing, such as aerosols, are neglected. Therefore, when we state that CO2 is the fundamental driver of long-term ITF changes, we do not exclude the possibility that direct causes may involve other dynamic or thermodynamic factors. However, at least in the CMIP6 model simulations, increasing CO2 remains the root cause of the ITF's long-term evolution.

What's more, we have examined the ITF response to CO2 in the SSP585 as well, through stitching the Hist runs to their corresponding SSP585 members. The ITF in the SSP585 scenarios shows a rapid decline after the first turning point, the rate of change in ITF transport as a function of CO2 concentration between the two turning points is much more rapid than before the first turning point or after the second turning point, indicating that the ITF response in the SSP585 is the same as that in the 1pctCO2 experiments (Supplementary Fig. S8). However, regardless, it must be noted that these results are numerical simulations under ideal conditions and carry significant uncertainties. The CMIP models seem to contain significant uncertainty in simulating the salinity variability 48 . Given that the salinity variability under global warming is potentially important, improving the skill in our ability to simulate salinity-related processes in climate models is clearly required.

Methods

Data

Model outputs from the historical runs (Hist, 1850-2014) and idealized 1% annually increasing CO2 concentrations experiments (1pctCO2, 1850-1999) from 25 CMIP6 coupled climate models 49 are used to investigate the response of ITF to external forcing. These models include: ACCESS-CM2, ACCESS-ESM1-5, CAMS-CSM1-0, CAS-ESM2-0, CESM2, CESM2-WACCM, CESM2-WACCM-FV2, CMCC-CM2-SR5, CMCC-ESM2, CanESM5, E3SM-1-0, EC-Earth3-AerChen, EC-Earth3-CC, EC-Earth3-Verg, FGOALS-f3-L, FIO-ESM-2-0, IPSL-CM5A2-INCA, IPSLCM6A-LR, MCM-UA-1-0, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-HAM, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MRI-ESM2-0, NESM3, and SAM0-UNICON. The 1pctCO2 is one of the four baseline experiments of the Diagnostic, Evaluation and Characterization of Klima (DECK) experiments, which is one of the three major components of CMIP. We used temperature, salinity, zonal and meridional current velocities, zonal and meridional components of wind stress, and precipitation from these models to investigate the responses of the halosteric and thermosteric components of the ITF to the increasing CO2 forcing.

Both the Hist and 1pctCO2 runs branch the end of the preindustrial control (piControl runs) in 1850. The piControl reproduces an equilibrium state that lasts 500 years and serves as a baseline for branched experiments like the Hist and 1pctCO2. The forcing for Hist and 1pctCO2 runs are external forcing like emissions of greenhouse gases (GHGs) and aerosols due to human activities and volcanic eruptions. It should be noted that the external forcing for the Hist runs are based on observations over 1850-2014 50 , and the Hist runs are usually used to examine natural variability and compare with historical observations 17 . For example, Santoso et al. 17 found that the CMIP5 models overall simulate aspects of ITF variability. The 1pctCO2 runs branches the piControl as well, but are driven by idealized forcing that is the same as that in piControl except for the prescribed CO2 concentration increasing at 1% yr -1 (month by month or suddenly rises by 1% at the end of the year) since 1850 without considering any observations. So, the historical runs can be approximately regarded as an approximation of "real world", while the 1pctCO2 runs are idealized experiments. Using the 1pctCO2 runs and their comparison with Hist runs, we are able to emulate the response of the ITF to an increasing of CO2 concentration.

In addition to the Hist runs and 1pctCO2 runs, simulations from the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) 585 and forced by an abrupt quadrupling of CO2 (abrupt 4×CO2 runs) when available are employed as well. The geostrophic transport of ITF from Lu et al. 30 (L24) is used for comparison to the numerical simulations. The geostrophic transport L24 was estimated using observation-based dataset products.
