Ocean-atmosphere feedbacks key to NAO decadal predictability

Check for updates

Casey R. Patrizio 1 , Panos J. Athanasiadis 1 , DougM. Smith 2 &Dario Nicolì 1

Evidence has emerged that the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) may be predictable on decadal timescales, which may greatly benefit society given the significant climate impacts that accompany the NAO. However, the mechanisms behind the apparent decadal predictability of the NAO, including the role of ocean-atmosphere interactions, have not yet been pinned down. In this study, the decadal prediction skill for the NAO and the interactions with the underlying ocean are assessed in retrospective forecasts spanning 1960-2020 using eight different decadal prediction systems (DPSs) and observation-based data. We find considerable spread in NAO skill across the DPSs and critically, that this is linked to differences in the representation of ocean-NAO interactions between the systems. Evidence is shown that NAO skill is related to positive feedback between subpolar SSTs and the NAO, which varies in strength between DPSs yet may still be too weak even in the most skillful systems compared to an observational estimate. We also report evidence that the positive feedback is opposed by a delayed negative feedback between the NAO and the ocean circulation, which is used to further explain the disparities in NAO skill across systems. Our findings, therefore, suggest that North Atlantic ocean-atmosphere interactions are central to NAO decadal predictability.

The dominant pattern of atmospheric variability over the North Atlantic, the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO), has long been viewed as unpredictable due to chaotic dynamics inherent to the extratropical atmosphere, but recent studies have shown that the NAO exhibits significant predictability on seasonal to decadal timescales 1-8 . Such variations in the NAO have significant climate impacts, especially over Europe, via associated changes in the extratropical jet stream 9,10 , and hence society may profoundly benefit from skillful predictions of the NAO. However, questions remain regarding the mechanisms underlying NAO predictability, particularly on the decadal timescale 5,8 . An improved understanding of these mechanisms is an essential step towards addressing outstanding issues in NAO predictions, including the unrealistically weak signal-to-noise ratio of the NAO in prediction systems 11,12 .

Previous studies suggest that the decadal predictability of the NAO, as demonstrated in retrospective forecasts of the last few decades 7,8,12,13 , may arise from its interactions with the North Atlantic 8,14 . In particular, the NAO signal (i.e., its predictable component) has been linked to a pattern of seasurface temperature (SST) anomalies in the North Atlantic that resembles the dominant pattern of atlantic multidecadal variability (AMV) 8 , which may affect the extratropical atmospheric circulation via meridional gradients in SST that in turn influence the eddy-driven jet 8,14-18 . However, the precise mechanisms governing the influence of North Atlantic SST anomalies on the extratropical atmospheric circulation are not fully understood, partly because the extratropical atmospheric response to midlatitude SST anomalies is obscured by the strong internal variability of the extratropical atmosphere 19 , and partly because simulated atmospheric responses in model experiments seemingly depend on the specific model and resolution that is used 20-22 .

The relationship between the North Atlantic and the decadal NAO signal may also be viewed as a two-way interaction, as the NAO is known to drive oceanic variability through various processes 23 . Most notably, the NAO affects the upper-ocean via surface heat flux anomalies (latent, sensible, and radiative), yielding red-noise variability in SSTs due to the ocean mixed-layer's large heat capacity 24,25 . For example, surface heat fluxes associated with the positive phase of the NAO cool the subpolar North Atlantic region 25-27 . The NAO may also influence oceanic variability on long timescales via its impacts on ocean circulation 26,28 . Specifically, NAO-related surface wind-stress anomalies can drive delayed responses of the ocean gyres that evolve on annual timescales 28,29 , and NAO-related surface heat flux anomalies can drive delayed responses in the subpolar North Atlantic gyre and Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) that evolve on decadal timescales 30-32 . Whether such mechanisms are relevant to decadal NAO variability in the real world remains to be determined, in large part due to the limited observational record of the ocean circulation and an incomplete understanding of the ocean's influence on the NAO. Despite these limitations, there is strong evidence that ocean circulation plays a key

Given the complex range of atmosphere-ocean interactions that may contribute to the prediction skill for the NAO, it is plausible that key processes are misrepresented by models. Indeed, model deficiencies in low-frequency SST variability in the North Atlantic and the NAO have been reported in previous studies 37-41 . Notably, it has also been shown that models underestimate the connection between North Atlantic SSTs and the extratropical atmospheric circulation compared to observations 42,43 , highlighting a possible widespread misrepresentation of the two-way feedback between the NAO and North Atlantic SSTs. Importantly, if atmosphere-ocean interactions are indeed key to NAO decadal predictability, such model deficiencies would be expected to limit the predictive skill of the NAO.

Here, we assess the predictive skill of the NAO and the interactions with the underlying ocean in retrospective forecasts of the last few decades (referred to as "decadal hindcasts") with eight decadal prediction systems (DPSs) from the Decadal Climate Prediction Project (dcppA-hindcast experiments, Methods) 44 . We show that there is considerable spread in NAO skill across the DPSs and, critically, that this is linked to differences in the representation of ocean-NAO feedback between the systems. Our findings, therefore, emphasize the importance of accurately representing North Atlantic ocean-atmosphere interactions in producing skillful decadal predictions of the NAO.

Results

Decadal skill for NAO and North Atlantic SSTs

Out of the eight DPSs, three systems (CMCC, CESM, and HadGEM) exhibit statistically significant anomaly correlation coefficients (ACC) for the NAO in winter (DJFM) after detrending (solid markers in Fig. 1a, Methods).

Hereafter, we refer to these systems as the "skillful DPSs" and the remaining systems as the "skill-less DPSs". We calculated the ACC for the period 1960-2021 by correlating the ensemble-mean NAO in each DPS averaged over forecast-years (FYs) 1-8 with an 8-year running average of the observed NAO computed with ERA5 reanalysis 45 . Further details on the NAO definition, the assessment of skill, and statistical significance testing are provided in Methods.

The significant ACCs for the NAO in the skillful DPSs (Fig. 1a) are commensurate with values from previous studies that have reported decadal skill for the NAO in these systems 5,7,8,12,13 , but our findings critically indicate that not all systems exhibit such skill. Notably, the disparities in NAO skill across DPSs cannot be explained by the larger ensemble sizes of CMCC (20 members) and CESM DPSs (40 members) compared to the other DPSs (10 members, except for CanESM, which has 20 members). To provide a fair comparison, we calculated ACCs for the CMCC and CESM DPSs by averaging over 500 random combinations of 10-member sub-ensembles (circles) and found that the higher ACCs remain significant in both systems. As expected 8 , the NAO skill in these systems is further enhanced when using their full ensembles (asterisks).

A recent study argued that the NAO skill reported in decadal hindcasts may be largely driven by external radiative forcings 46 . Here we remove the trend in order to focus on the decadal variability of the NAO, which is substantial compared to the trend over the 1960-2020 period, but recognize that non-linear variations in external radiative forcings, particularly those associated with anthropogenic aerosol emissions, may contribute to decadal variations in the NAO 46 . Therefore, for comparison, we also calculated the detrended ACC for ensemble means of the respective 10-member uninitialized historical simulations (for CMCC and CESM but not HadGEM, which only has 4 historical members available; see Methods) for the same period. Overall, in agreement with previous studies 8 , Fig. 1a suggests that the initialization of the ocean and possibly other components of the climate system (like sea-ice) may be key to the decadal skill for the NAO. In the rest of the study, we explore whether the decadal skill for the NAO is related to interactions between the underlying ocean and the NAO. Figure 1b shows that North Atlantic SSTs exhibit significant skill in most DPSs, especially in the subtropical and subpolar regions. However, the skillful DPSs generally exhibit the greatest ACC for SSTs in the subpolar region (light blue markers in Fig. 1a), which suggests this region may be key to NAO decadal predictability. Decadal anomalies in SSTs and upper-ocean heat content in the subpolar North Atlantic have been shown to be highly predictable thanks to ocean initialization and dynamics associated with the subpolar gyre and the AMOC 34,35 . Importantly, however, our findings suggest that this decadal skill for subpolar SSTs may be related to the decadal skill for the NAO and, hence, may not arise solely from ocean dynamics.

Influence of Subpolar SSTs on the NAO

The apparent link between skill for subpolar SSTs and skill for the NAO, as suggested by Fig. 1, does not necessarily indicate a one-way relationship (SST skill leading to NAO skill) as it is well-known that the NAO drives subpolar SST anomalies via surface heat fluxes 25-27 . In order to better identify the influence of the ocean on the atmosphere, Fig. 2a shows the correlation between winter (DJFM) sea-level pressure (SLP) anomalies and subpolar SST anomalies during the preceding autumn season (SON). This seasonal lag helps isolate the oceanic influence on the extratropical atmospheric circulation, which is expected to occur on a monthly timescale 47 . Interestingly, warm subpolar SSTs during autumn are correlated in a statistically significant sense with a winter SLP pattern resembling the negative phase of the NAO in the skillful DPSs, but not in the skill-less DPSs.

The SLP correlation pattern in ERA5 (leftmost panel of Fig. 2a) is qualitatively similar to that in the skillful DPSs, but exhibits much stronger values and resembles more closely the NAO pattern. However, the comparison between ERA5 and the DPSs is not entirely fair because ERA5 timeseries constitute a single realization and are calculated from runningaveraged fields that are continuous in time (Methods), whereas the results for the DPSs shown here are derived from ensemble-mean FY averaged fields that are partly discontinuous in time (forecasts initialized on successive years are all different). To address this disparity, we compared ERA5 with individual members from uninitialized historical simulations (continuous in time) conducted with the same models used in the DPSs and treated the same exact way as ERA5. Importantly, large areas of the observed SLP correlations for the DPSs were found to exceed the correlations found in any of the members of the respective historical simulations (green hatching), which suggests that the forcing of the winter NAO by subpolar SST anomalies is stronger in observations than the models analyzed here, consistent with recent studies 40,42 . We show later that similar results hold also for individual members from the decadal hindcasts.

The forcing of the NAO by North Atlantic SSTs, as suggested by Fig. 2a, and its possible role in NAO decadal predictability is supported by findings from previous studies 8,14-16,48-50 . Here, it is further demonstrated that not all DPSs simulate such a relationship and that, critically, this is linked to differences in the decadal skill for the NAO across various DPSs. Furthermore, given the well-established influence of the NAO on subpolar SSTs 25 , these results also suggest that the decadal SST signal may feed back onto the decadal NAO signal. First, we present evidence of the influence of the subpolar SST signal on the NAO signal, and later, evidence of the NAO signal on the subpolar SST signal, closing the feedback loop.

To quantify the potential SST-NAO feedback (i.e., influence of subpolar SST on the NAO), we calculated the correlation between autumn subpolar SSTs and the winter NAO, denoted by rSST, NAO, for the DPSs and for ERA5. As shown in Fig. 2b, rSST, NAO is generally negative in all systems, which agrees in sign with the respective correlation when the NAO drives SSTs (i.e., the positive phase of the NAO is associated with stronger heat fluxes out of the subpolar ocean giving rise to cold SST anomalies there). Since cold subpolar SSTs lead a positive NAO (as in Fig. 2a but considering the opposite polarity) this is indicative of a positive feedback. However, the magnitude of rSST, NAO is generally larger in skillful DPSs (orange markers in Fig. 2b) and statistically significant (particularly for CMCC and CESM), while it is weaker and not statistically significant for the skill-less DPSs (green markers). The difference in rSST, NAO between the two groups of DPSs is also supported by correlations calculated from larger 60-member ensemble-means formed from combining all skillful DPSs (orange diamond marker in Fig. 2b) and all skill-less DPSs (green diamond marker in Fig. 2b; see Methods).

Consistent with Fig. 2a, the respective rSST, NAO from ERA5 (around -0.8, as shown in Fig. 2b) is much greater in magnitude than the DPSs and lies outside the spread defined by all individual members of the historical simulations (whiskers). Although the observed rSST, NAO contains some noise (only a component of the observed NAO variability is thought to be predictable and forced by SSTs), these findings suggest that the positive feedback may be underestimated by models. We suspect that this model deficiency may contribute to the weak signal-to-noise ratio for the NAO in decadal hindcasts 7,8,12 and hence hinder the decadal predictability of the NAO. Indeed, NAO skill is found to exhibit strong statistical association with rSST, NAO across the DPS ensemble-means (linear correlation r = -0.82; see color markers in Fig. 2c), which suggests that greater positive feedback strengths may lead to greater decadal skill for the NAO. Note that the results in Fig. 2b are qualitatively similar to those of the NAO index (unstandardized) regressed onto the subpolar SST index (Supplementary Fig. 2) with ERA5 and skillful DPSs exhibiting larger amplitude NAO-like anomalies in association with autumn subpolar SSTs compared to the skill-less DPSs, except with even larger disparities between ERA5 and the models (including individual members of the historical simulations), further indicating a significant model deficiency and possible contributing factor to the signal-tonoise problem for the NAO.

ERA5 and the uninitialized historical simulations are directly comparable, but these simulations are not constrained by observations as the DPSs are and therefore may be more prone to larger model biases and substantial deficiencies in North Atlantic variability 37,39,51-53 . To address this, we used a method to facilitate more direct comparison between ERA5 and the DPSs involving the analysis of the NAO-ocean interactions in 10,000 individual member combinations (IMCs) that for each DPS were randomly selected among all the possible forecast-member combinations forming complete timeseries (see Methods). In this way, ERA5 and the IMCs from each DPS both contain noise and predictable components of variability and so the comparison between the two is fairer than the comparison with the DPS ensemble means.

The gray markers in Fig. 2c indicate rSST, NAO and the ACC for the NAO for all IMCs from the most skillful DPS (CMCC). Notably, these two variables also exhibit a statistical association across the IMCs (linear correlation r ~ -0.6), with larger ACCs associated with more negative rSST, NAO, as is the case for the DPS ensemble-means (colored markers in Fig. 2c). This relationship is similar for IMCs from the other skillful DPSs (CESM and HadGEM), but is much weaker in the skill-less DPSs (Supplementary Fig. 3), which is expected from the lower skill for subpolar SSTs and the possibly deficient SST-NAO feedback in these systems. Importantly, the observed rSST, NAO remains far outside of the IMC spread of rSST, NAO (~-0.4 to 0.2), which supports our hypothesis that the SST-NAO feedback is underestimated by the DPSs. One may argue that ERA5 and IMCs may still not be fairly compared in this way because the ERA5 fields were smoothed with a running average while the IMC fields were averaged over forecast time, which introduces greater autocorrelation to the ERA5 fields. Two-way interactions between the NAO and SSTs

Decadal SST anomalies associated with the NAO. The findings in the previous section highlight the influence of subpolar SSTs on the NAO and its role in NAO decadal predictability. As previously discussed, this is indicative of a direct feedback between the NAO signal and subpolar SSTs because the NAO is also well-known to drive subpolar SST anomalies via the surface heat fluxes 25-27 . However, ensemble averaging is expected to substantially diminish the amplitude of the NAO and, hence, the associated surface heat flux anomalies. Thus, based on the findings presented so far, the decadal skill for the NAO could, in principle, be explained by a one-way response of the NAO to the subpolar (initialized) SSTs without any significant feedback between them.

To demonstrate the influence of the NAO signal on subpolar SSTs and hence provide further evidence of positive feedback, here we quantify the two-way interactions between the decadal NAO signal and the underlying decadal SST variability by regressing 8-year averaged winter-mean SST, SLP, and surface heat flux (SHF) anomalies onto the 8-year averaged NAO (Fig. 3). Therefore, in contrast to the previous section where the leading influence of (autumn) SSTs on the decadal (winter) NAO signal was quantified, the results discussed here correspond to anomalies that are concurrent with the NAO decadal signal and hence include both the oceanic response to the NAO as well as the oceanic influence on the NAO.

Further insights into the role of SSTs in decadal NAO predictability are provided by comparing regression patterns calculated from skillful DPS ensemble-mean fields with those calculated from skill-less DPS ensemblemean fields (Fig. 3c, d). We also calculated SST regressions for IMCs (Fig. 3b, c) in order to facilitate a more fair comparison between the DPSs and ERA5 results (Fig. 3a). In particular, regressions were calculated for IMCs in the 99th percentile of the ACC for the NAO (hereafter referred to as "top members"; see darker gray markers in Fig. 2c) and IMCs in the 1st percentile of the ACC for the NAO (hereafter referred to as "bottommembers"; also darker gray markers in Fig. 2c). The regression patterns are assessed in the top members to quantify how well the DPS reproduces the observed patterns when the IMCs are constrained to follow the observed NAO more closely. Top and bottom members are also compared (like skillful and skillless DPSs) to better understand which aspects of the NAO-SST interactions are most relevant to NAO decadal predictability.

Figure 3d indicates that skillful DPSs exhibit cold SST anomalies and positive surface heat fluxes in the subpolar region concurrently with the positive phase of the NAO (hereafter denoted NAO + ). This suggests that the NAO signal indeed affects the subpolar SST signal via surface heat flux anomalies in the skillful DPSs, closing the positive feedback loop as discussed in the previous section. Furthermore, the skill-less DPSs do not exhibit significant cold SST anomalies in association with NAO + (Fig. 3e), despite being associated with similar magnitude SLP

anomalies and only slightly weaker surface heat flux anomalies. Thus, consistent with the results highlighted in Fig. 2, the results presented here indicate that subpolar SSTs are linked to the NAO in skillful DPSs but not in skill-less DPSs, highlighting a clear disparity in two-way interactions between these systems (i.e., both in terms of the response of the ocean to the NAO, as well as the response of the NAO to the ocean). In later sections, we further assess the ocean response to the NAO in skillful and skill-less DPSs in order to better understand this disparity. Note that Fig. 3d indicates that subtropical SST anomalies are also related to the NAO signal in skillful DPSs, the disparities in the NAOSST relationship between skillful and skill-less DPSs are generally larger in the subpolar region, which suggests that extratropical interactions may be key to NAO decadal predictability. Finally, while the magnitude of the NAO signal is similar in the skillful and skill-less DPSs (bottom row in Fig. 3d, e), the autocorrelation and, thus persistence of the NAO signal is significantly greater in the skillful DPSs (Fig. 4), as might be expected from the stronger SST-NAO feedback in these systems.

The significant disparities in the NAO-subpolar SST relationship between skillful and skill-less DPSs are notably similar for top members vs. bottommembers from the CMCC DPSs (Fig. 3b, c), which indicates that the dynamics in IMCs are relevant to NAO predictability and thus motivates further analysis of IMCs in later sections. The NAO-related SST, SHF, and SLP anomalies in top members are considerably larger than for ensemble means from the skillful DPSs because IMCs contain both noise and predictable components of variability. However, even top members do not exhibit NAO-related anomalies as large as those shown in ERA5 (Fig. 3a), which suggests that the relationship between the NAO and the underlying SSTs on the decadal timescale is underestimated by the DPSs and hence is consistent with an underestimated positive feedback. Indeed, the magnitude of the correlation between the 8-year averaged NAO and underlying SST anomalies in ERA5 exceeds that of all members from the uninitialized historical simulations in the subpolar region and parts of the subtropics (green hatching in Fig. 3a).

Evolution of SSTs associated with the NAO. Previous studies have shown that slow interactions between the NAO and North Atlantic SSTs via ocean circulation adjustments are likely important to North Atlantic decadal variability 26,54-57 and hence may play a role in decadal NAO predictability. Therefore, here we explore the role of such slow ocean processes by analyzing the evolution of SSTs along the 10-year forecasts in relation to the decadal NAO variability. To do so, annual-mean SST anomalies at each FY were regressed onto the 8-year mean NAO index. The SST evolution is analyzed both in ERA5 and IMCs, where "FY" for ERA5 refers to the year relative to that used in the running average of the NAO.

Figure 5 shows the annual SST anomalies at each FY regressed onto the 8-year mean NAO for ERA5, as well as for the top and bottom members from the CMCC DPS. During the initial FYs (FYs 1-5), ERA5 exhibits cold SST anomalies in the subpolar and subtropical regions, and warm SST anomalies in a narrow region along the Gulf Stream front in association with NAO + (Fig. 5a). Over time, however, the cold anomalies decay and are eventually replaced by warm (but weak) SST anomalies by FY 10. Warm SST anomalies also appear to propagate northeastward along the Gulf Stream extension and eventually into the subpolar and subtropical regions, which indicates that the delayed warming may be related to the oceanic circulation. The top members from the CMCC DPS exhibit a similar relationship, but with weaker cold anomalies in the first FYs and stronger warm anomalies during later FYs (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, as in Figs. 2 and 3, none of the members from the uninitialized historical simulations have SST-NAO correlations with magnitudes as large as in ERA5 in both the subpolar and subtropical North Atlantic during the initial FYs (green hatching in Fig. 5a). Interestingly, the bottommembers also exhibit the warm anomalies at later FYs, but not the initial cold anomalies seen in the top members and ERA5 (Fig. 5c).

Figure 5 therefore suggests that a positive feedback between subpolar SSTs and the NAO is active in ERA5 and the top members (but stronger in ERA5) during the initial FYs when the cold subpolar SST anomalies are present. However, the delayed subpolar warming present in ERA5 and all members act as negative feedback on the NAO + since it tends to drive a negative phase of the NAO (Fig. 2b). These results suggest both positive and negative feedbacks are active in the top members, but only the negative feedback is active in the bottommembers. Hence, consistent with the results shown in Fig. 3, in the bottommembers, the decadal NAO variability does not seem to be related to positive feedback with subpolar SSTs, and thus, being determined more strongly by chaotic atmospheric variability than in top members is more poorly correlated with the observed NAO variability. In contrast, in the top members the decadal NAO variability is amplified via the positive feedback and consequently contains a stronger predictable component contributing to higher NAO skill. Indeed, ensemble-means from the CMCC DPS exhibit a qualitatively similar SST evolution to that of the top members shown in Fig. 5b (Supplementary Fig. 5a), which indicates that these interactions are relevant to the predictable component of the NAO.

Role of AMOC. Modeling studies have shown that the NAO can drive delayed warming or cooling of the subpolar region via the ocean circulation response to surface heat fluxes 28-32,54,57 . In particular, the NAO + is associated with anomalous surface cooling in the subpolar region, which promotes deep convection there and, hence, AMOC strengthening that eventually leads to increased ocean heat transport into this region 32 . Here, we show that such an AMOC response may explain the delayed subpolar warming seen in both the top and bottom members of the CMCC DPS (Fig. 5b, c).

As shown by Fig. 6, the NAO + is associated with positive AMOC anomalies in the extratropics that tend to grow in forecast time, especially in the top members (Fig. 6a). This is consistent with the gradual decay in cold subpolar SST anomalies and the warming during the later FYs. The bottom members exhibit a similar pattern, but with overturning anomalies that span more of the North Atlantic also during the initial FYs. These positive AMOC anomalies are again expected to drive extratropical warming and hence are consistent with the lack of cold anomalies in bottommembers during the initial FYs (Fig. 5c). In either case, the results suggest that the AMOC response to decadal NAO anomalies may act as a negative feedback to the NAO in the CMCC DPS via the associated anomalous ocean heat transport into the subpolar gyre.

SST Evolution in Skillful vs. Skill-less DPSs. To assess robustness we extend the IMC analysis to all DPSs examined in this study. Figure 7a shows the subpolar SSTs at each FY regressed onto the 8-year mean NAO for ERA5 (black line), the top members from the skillful DPSs (orange), and the top members from the skill-less DPSs (green). Similar plots are shown for the AMOC regressed onto the NAO, subpolar SSTs regressed onto SHFs, and the evolution of the NAO amplitude in Fig. 7b-d, respectively. The top members are examined here in order to assess how well the DPSs capture the observed interactions when IMCs are constrained to follow the observed NAO as much as possible for each DPS (the observed NAO is similarly correlated with the NAO in top members from skillful and skill-less DPS, with multi-model mean ACCs of about 0.4 and 0.3, respectively).

Notably, the preceding results for top vs. bottommembers from the CMCC DPS (Fig. 5) are generally consistent with the results for top members from skillful DPSs vs. top members from skill-less DPSs shown here (orange and green lines, respectively, in Fig. 7). In particular, the top members from skillful DPSs initially exhibit cold subpolar SST anomalies that decay over time (orange line in Fig. 7a) and this appears consistent with the delayed AMOC response to the NAO + (orange line Fig. 7b) inducing a positive ocean heat transport anomaly.

The initial cold subpolar SST anomalies are weaker in the skill-less DPSs (green line in Fig. 7a), which is consistent with both the weaker positive feedback in these systems and the positive AMOC anomalies that are present also during the initial FYs (green line Fig. 7b). This is in contrast to the positive AMOC anomalies in the skillful DPSs that emerge only during later FYs. The possible causes of the different AMOC behavior were not investigated but could be related to deficiencies in ocean initialization (e.g., NAO + events may be unrealistically initialized with positive AMOC anomalies in skill-less DPSs), and/or in the AMOC response to the NAO between systems (e.g., the AMOC response may be too quick in the skill-less DPSs). In either case, the persistent positive AMOC anomalies in the skillless DPSs are expected to drive subpolar warming that offsets the subpolar cooling due to surface heat fluxes associated with the NAO + (Fig. 7c, d), and hence may inhibit the positive feedback between subpolar SSTs and the NAO in these systems.

Thus, these results yet again indicate that the models analyzed here may underestimate both the subpolar SST response to the NAO as well as the NAO-like response to subpolar SSTs and hence the two-way feedback. Finally, it is interesting that the ERA5 SST anomalies increase in magnitude over this time (LY1-3) as might be expected from a relatively stronger positive feedback in observations. A hint of this behavior is seen in the skillful DPSs (initial deepening of the orange line), but not in the skillless DPSs.

These results are further supported by subpolar SSTs regressed onto subpolar surface heat flux (SHF) anomalies averaged over FYs 1-8 (Fig. 7c). Positive subpolar SHF anomalies (i.e., heat fluxes out of the ocean) are associated with cold SST anomalies in ERA5 and to a much lesser extent, the top members from the skillful DPSs during the initial FYs, which reflects the atmospheric forcing of subpolar SSTs in either case and a relatively stronger atmospheric forcing in ERA5. However, the top members from skill-less DPSs do not exhibit such a relationship, which is consistent with a greater role for ocean circulation in driving subpolar SSTs during the initial FYs in these systems. Such clear disparities in the subpolar SST-SHF relationship between the models and ERA5 indicate broad model deficiencies in the coupling between the extratropical atmosphere and subpolar SSTs, consistent with the arguments put forth in this study. Note that the results for 60-member ensemble-means are qualitatively similar to the results for top members shown here, emphasizing the relevance of these findings to NAO predictability, except with a weaker signature of atmospheric forcing (and relatively stronger oceanic forcing) of subpolar SSTs in both skillful and skill-less DPSs as is expected from reduced NAO variance due to ensembleaveraging (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c).

The top members in skillful and skill-less DPSs exhibit NAO-related SLP anomalies of comparable amplitude at each FY (Fig. 7d), and thus the disparities in the two-way NAO-ocean interactions between the DPSs discussed above cannot be explained in terms of differences in the strength of the wind forcing between the systems. Interestingly, ERA5 is yet again an outlier compared to the models at most other FYs in this regard, with the magnitude of the NAO-related SLP anomalies exceeding the respective magnitude in the top members from all DPSs and that of all members from the historical simulations (black markers outlined in yellow markers in Fig. 7d). This suggests that the low-frequency variance of the NAO is underestimated by the models, which is consistent with findings from previous studies 41-43 and could be a consequence of the underestimated positive feedback as argued here.

Considering that the NAO predictability in the skillful DPSs is expected to stem from ocean initialization, it is worth determining which aspect of the initial oceanic states carries the associated predictable signal. Investigating this important question goes beyond the scope of the present study, nevertheless some relevant observations are made here. Since AMOC anomalies in FY1 are near zero (orange line in Fig. 7b), we suspect that the NAO signal may be related to other components of the ocean circulation (e.g. the barotropic gyre circulation) and/or initial anomalies in upper-ocean heat content in the subpolar North Atlantic. Given that the subpolar region has a relatively deep mixed layer, the latter hypothesis is consistent with SST anomalies already present for skillful DPSs in FY1 (e.g. orange line in Fig. 7a).

Discussion

In this study, we show that the decadal predictability of the NAO 7,8,12,13 is related to two-way interactions between the NAO and the underlying ocean.

Based on the analysis of decadal hindcasts from eight different DPSs, evidence is found that NAO skill is related to positive feedback between subpolar North Atlantic SSTs but that the feedback is too weak even in the most skillful DPSs compared to an observation-based estimate from ERA5. We further show that this deficiency is related to both parts of the feedback loop: the atmospheric response to subpolar SSTs, but also the response of the underlying ocean to the NAO. The findings support a growing body of evidence suggesting that the NAO and associated SST anomalies may reinforce each other via feedback on fast (monthly) timescales 22,50,58-61 . The results are also consistent with previous studies showing a link between North Atlantic SSTs and the NAO signal in hindcasts 8,14 and other studies showing that climate models tend to underestimate multidecadal variability in North Atlantic SSTs and the extratropical atmospheric circulation 39-42 in association with an underestimated ocean-atmosphere connection 42,43 . Our results suggest that the underestimated positive feedback in the DPSs contributes to the signal-to-noise deficit for the NAO in decadal hindcasts 11,12 , and hence the NAO predictability is likely underestimated, even in the best-performing current DPSs.

The specific mechanisms behind the apparent NAO-like response to subpolar SSTs were not investigated here, but previous studies offer a few key insights. Recent findings based on a seasonal prediction system suggest that subpolar North Atlantic SSTs impact the NAO via their impact on meridional SST gradients, which in turn affect the North Atlantic jet stream speed 14 . This is supported by previous studies showing more generally that North Atlantic SST anomalies can impact the eddy-driven jet via their impact on meridional gradients in low-level baroclinicity 8,15-18 . However, model experiments also show that the simulated extratropical atmospheric response to North Atlantic SST anomalies depends on the model that is used, with some responses projecting more strongly onto the NAO than others 20,21 . In fact, the DPSs with significant decadal skill for the NAO reported here (CMCC, CESM, HadGEM) were also found in idealized model experiments to exhibit distinct downstream shifts of the eddy-driven jet in response to AMV-like SST anomalies, while other systems exhibited different atmospheric responses 21 .

Understanding the causes of model disparities in the atmospheric response to North Atlantic SST anomalies is a challenging task, yet may be key to understanding why DPSs seemingly under-represent the positive feedback between subpolar SSTs and the NAO. Possible causes include model biases in the extratropical atmosphere related to the North Atlantic eddy-driven jet 21 and eddy feedback 62 , but also in the underlying ocean, including in the mean SSTs 51 and mixed-layer depths 63 . Such mean biases (in the ocean and atmosphere) are expected to cause drifts in the decadal hindcasts, which may interfere with the interactions between the NAO and North Atlantic SSTs on the decadal timescale, subsequently ruining the respective predictable signals. Deficiencies in the simulated patterns of internal variability in the atmosphere (e.g., related to the NAO and/or blocking) and the ocean (the AMOC, the Gulf Stream, and the wind-driven ocean gyres) may also adversely affect the feedback. Increased model resolution has been shown to mitigate some of these deficiencies, particularly in the extratropical atmospheric response to Gulf Stream variability 22,64 , but there is also evidence that increased atmospheric resolution alone only leads to minimal improvements in NAO predictive skill 65 . Further targeted multi-model studies are needed to pin down the origins of model deficiencies in the extratropical atmospheric circulation response to North Atlantic SST anomalies and the associated positive feedback.

We also present evidence that NAO decadal predictability is influenced by negative feedback involving the ocean circulation. We show that positive decadal NAO anomalies are associated with delayed subpolar warming in observations and in DPSs with significant skill for the NAO, which is related to a multi-year lagged AMOC response to the NAO in these systems. Since the delayed subpolar warming opposes the initial subpolar cooling that acts to reinforce positive NAO anomalies, we argue that the AMOC acts as a delayed negative feedback to the NAO, consistent with previous studies 56,60,66 . In contrast, the DPSs without significant skill for the NAO exhibit positive AMOC anomalies and hence negative feedback throughout the entire 10-year hindcast period in association with the positive NAO.

This different AMOC behavior may be related to disparities in ocean initialization and/or the AMOC response to the NAO between the two groups of DPSs, but this should be investigated in future studies. Regardless of the cause, it is hypothesized that these persistent positive AMOC anomalies may contribute to the reduced strength of the positive SST-NAO feedback in these systems via the associated subpolar warming and, relatedly, may promote less persistent NAO variability compared to the DPSs exhibiting significant skill for the NAO. Although the AMOC appears to play a key role here, other aspects of the ocean circulation may be relevant to NAO predictability and should be explored further, particularly the gyre response to wind-stress anomalies associated with the NAO 55,57,67 .

Previous modeling studies have shown that delayed ocean circulationNAO feedbacks are key to decadal North Atlantic variability, and in some cases may even promote oscillations between the ocean and the NAO on subdecadal to multidecadal timescales 50,54,56,60,66,68 . Due to the limited observational record of the ocean circulation, we cannot definitively conclude whether the ocean circulation plays a similar role in reality. However, our findings provide evidence that both the delayed oceanic circulation response to the NAO and the fast extratropical atmospheric circulation response to North Atlantic variability are key aspects of the observed NAO decadal variability, which together are expected to favor oscillatory behavior. In addition, our results indicate that model deficiencies in these two-way ocean-atmosphere interactions may limit the decadal prediction skill for the NAO in current DPSs.

The value of ocean initialization is highlighted by the significant increase in decadal skill for the NAO in the CMCC and CESM DPSs compared to the respective uninitialized historical simulations. While the decadal NAO skill may partly derive from the linear trend associated with external radiative forcings 46 , the trend has been removed here to isolate the significant decadal NAO variability. Hence, it is possible that the remaining decadal NAO variability could be related to internal mechanisms, but we also cannot rule out the possibility that the model oceanic response to timevarying external radiative forcings is deficient and that this is partly mitigated by ocean initialization 12,69 . Regardless of whether the decadal variability is internally or externally driven, our findings emphasize the importance of ocean initialization and the accurate representation of twoway ocean-NAO interactions in producing skillful decadal predictions of the NAO. Determining which aspect of the initial ocean state matters most for the NAO signal is fundamentally important for advancing the understanding of the source of NAO decadal predictability and is the subject of ongoing work. Nevertheless, future improvements in ocean initialization or the model representation of extratropical atmosphere-ocean interactions, for example, through improved ocean observations or increased model resolution, are expected to enhance the decadal prediction skill for the NAO.

To produce the decadal hindcasts, model simulations were realistically initialized every November (except for CanESM and IPSL, which were initialized in January, and NorCPM, which was initialized in October) and then run for 10 years. For each start date, multiple simulations were performed with perturbed initial conditions to provide a range of possible decadal forecasts (referred to as ensemble members or simply members). Significant NAO skill is only achieved when averaging over a sufficiently large ensemble, which reduces the unpredictable variability (noise) and hence allows the predictable signal (conceptually common to all members) to emerge. Hence, we analyzed only the DPSs that provide at least 10 members to help sufficiently reduce the noise.

Ensemble-means for each DPS were calculated using either 10 members, or the full ensembles for those systems with more members. The 10-member ensembles for CMCC, CESM and CanESM5 were formed by randomly selecting 500 different combinations from the full ensembles. We also formed 60-member ensembles for the skill-less DPS by combining all members from the IPSL, MIROC, CanESM, ECEarth and NorCPMDPSs. For the skillful DPSs, we randomly selected 500 different combinations of 60 members out of the 70 total members from the CMCC, CESM and HadGEM DPSs while maintaining the relative ensemble sizes (i.e., by randomly selecting 17/34/9 members from each DPS, respectively). This was done in order to avoid overselecting members from DPSs with larger ensemble sizes.

From the hindcast output, SST (tos), net surface heat fluxes (hfds), SLP (psl), and meridional overturning circulation (msftyz or msftmz depending on availability) monthly-mean data were acquired and then averaged over winter (DJFM), autumn (SON) or the entire year. Winter, autumn or annual anomalies were calculated by subtracting the FY dependent climatology from the fields and then removing the linear trend. All fields were regridded to a regular 1° × 1° latitude-longitude grid prior to the analyses. Note that, in systems that are initialized in January, only JFM was used to calculated the winter-mean anomalies for the first FY.

To assess the NAO predictability and ocean-NAO interactions in the DPSs, we used observation-based data from the European Center for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Reanalysis v5 (ERA5) 45 , which spans a similar time period as the hindcasts (1959-2021). Decadal skill for the NAO was assessed by averaging the hindcast SLP fields over FYs 1-8 and applying an 8-year running-average to the ERA5 SLP fields. Given that SLP is well constrained in reanalyzes (particularly 1950 onward), there is very little observational uncertainty associated to this variable, and so comparing results for other atmospheric reanalysis products was not considered necessary.

Considering that ERA5 constitutes a single realization calculated from running-averaged fields that are continuous in time, whereas the results for the DPSs are derived from FY averaged fields that are partly discontinuous in time (i.e., hindcast simulations are run independently for each initialization year), we also analyzed uninitialized historical simulations treated the same way as ERA5. Monthly-mean output was analyzed from historical simulations following the CMIP6 protocol using the same eight models as the decadal hindcasts. We acquired 10 members for each model (except HadGEM-MM, which only had 4 members available) and analyzed the fields over a similar time period as the hindcasts (1960-2014). All simulations are forced with observed external forcings, which include greenhouse gas emissions, aerosol emissions, volcanic eruptions, and variations in solar activity.

NAO definition and skill assessment

The NAO index was defined as the difference in DJFM-mean SLP between 35°N and 65°N, which was zonally-averaged between 80°W and 30°E and standardized. This definition 70 accounts for model-to-model differences in the centers of action of the NAO across the DPSs, but the more traditional station-based definition 71 yields similar results. To isolate decadal variability, the NAO index was smoothed with an 8-year running average. Note that the NAO index was standardized to unit variance after the 8-year running average was applied.

The decadal skill for the NAO was assessed by calculating its ACC. We calculated the ACC by correlating the 8-year averaged NAO from ERA5 45 with the ensemble-mean NAO in each decadal hindcast averaged over FYs (LYs) 1-8. The FY averaging is essential for isolating the predictable component of the NAO 8 , and LYs 1-8 were chosen here because this captures most of the 10-year hindcast period, while maximizing the number of systems that exhibit statistically significant ACC for the NAO. However, the CMCC and CESM DPSs remain the leading systems for NAO skill when other FY averaging ranges are used (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Statistical significance testing

We evaluate the statistical significance of correlations and regressions for DPS ensemble means, individual members from the DPSs, and ERA5, using a non-parametric block bootstrapping method 12 . In this case, we resample with replacement the fields or indices being tested in 5-year blocks in order to account for autocorrelation. The required statistic (i.e., correlation or regression) was calculated, and the procedure was repeated 2000 times to generate a probability distribution. By construction the correlation or regression will be close to zero when averaged over all the samples, but will yield a distribution by chance. We consider the statistic to be significant if it is less than the 5th percentile or greater than the 95th percentile of this distribution. Note that this is equivalent to a two-tailed test of the null hypothesis that the statistic is zero with a p value of 0.1 or a 1-tailed test with a p value of 0.05.

Individual member combinations

IMCs were formed by randomly selecting a single member at each initialization year (e.g., member 1 for initialization year 1960, member 7 for initialization year 1961 ... and so on until the last initialization year). In this way, the IMC reflects a unique time evolution of a given field or index that is based on a single member and hence facilitates a fair comparison with results from the single realization of ERA5. By chance IMCs will sample a range of ACC for the NAO. We refer to "'top members" as the IMCs in the 99th percentile of the ACC for the NAO and "bottommembers" as the IMCs in the 1st percentile of the ACC for the NAO. The total number of IMCs that can be formed in this way is enormously large in practice (e.g. for a 10member hindcast with 60 initialization years, the number of possible combinations is ~10 60 ), so we limit our analysis to 10,000 combinations chosen randomly from the entire population. This number was chosen based on the convergence of the spread in ACC for the NAO calculated across IMCs, and hence, our results are robust to larger ensembles of IMCs.
